Introduction

In this lesson we will talk about Vector Space, Sub-Space and some basics of Matrices. This lesson is going to be more abstract and rigorous; this is so that it helps the reader in their academics while keeping in mind the reader might want to explore more abstract subjects from their genuine interest, like functional analysis on this site.

The idea of Vector Space and Sub-Space can be quite abstract, but here we will try to translate smoothly so that the reader can grasp the fundamental idea of Vector Space and Sub-Space.

Vectors

If you don't have a good grasp on the basics of vectors we recommend you first read our Lesson-1 where we discussed basics of vectors and matrices.

You may have heard the basic definition that a vector is a mathematical object that has a magnitude and a direction, and while it is true that vectors in two dimensions can be represented as arrows and thus the length of the arrow can be interpreted as the magnitude and the direction it is pointing toward is the direction, this is not really a mathematical definition but rather an interpretation of a certain type of vector. This simplistic view doesn't really hold up when we move to more abstract settings such as: polynomials, matrices, continuous functions, and infinite sequences — all of these can be considered vectors, and we can and in fact do work with them as vectors in abstract vector spaces. Thus we need to break free from the intuitive idea of vectors and submerge our feet into vector space, which will allow us to understand what a vector is more accurately.

In this section we will walk through various concepts that will allow us to arrive at a rigorous definition of a vector, starting with vector space.

Regardless, we will see the definition of a vector soon, but essentially vectors are mathematical objects, and one of the advantages they have is that they can be used to represent a wide variety of phenomena in different fields. Beyond pure mathematics, vectors are mostly used in Physics and Computer Science.

We shall first start by talking about the representation of vectors so that we can clear up some basic ideas about vectors, and then we will move on to more abstract concepts.

Representation of Vectors

Vectors in a finite-dimensional coordinate space are represented with some numbers \( (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) \) enclosed by brackets such as: \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}\), \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}\) or \(\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}\). These numbers can be either real or complex. From now on, unless we explicitly mention otherwise, we will draw the finite-dimensional vector from the origin of a chosen coordinate system to the point \( (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n) \). Not all vectors need be finite, nor do they have to be represented by a point on a coordinate system; here we are explicitly talking about finite-dimensional vectors to build our intuition so that the later abstractness still makes sense to us.

Column vectors and Row vectors

In coordinate linear algebra, vectors in \(\mathbb F^n\) are commonly represented as \(n\times 1\) column matrices, while \(1\times n\) row matrices are called row vectors. Here, \(\mathbb F\) denotes a field, such as \(\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\).

Column vectors are written vertically, with their components \(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n\) arranged from top to bottom, whereas row vectors are written horizontally. Both are matrices, but they play different roles in linear algebra.

Column vectors are the standard way of representing vectors in a coordinate vector space. More precisely, once we choose a basis for a vector space, we can represent each vector by a column containing its coordinates relative to that basis. This is a coordinate representation; an abstract vector space does not necessarily consist of tuples or matrices.

Row vectors have another important interpretation: they can represent linear functionals, which are elements of the dual space. A linear functional is a linear map \(f:\mathbb F^n\to\mathbb F\) that takes a vector as input and produces a scalar as output.

For example, consider a row vector \[ \mathbf a^T = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & \cdots & a_n \end{bmatrix} \] and a column vector \[ \mathbf x = \begin{bmatrix} x_1\\ x_2\\ \vdots\\ x_n \end{bmatrix}. \] Their matrix product is \[ \mathbf a^T\mathbf x = a_1x_1+a_2x_2+\cdots+a_nx_n. \] This is a scalar. In fact, the row vector defines a linear functional through the map \[ f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf a^T\mathbf x. \] Every linear functional on \(\mathbb F^n\) can be represented by a unique row vector once the standard coordinates are chosen.

For real coordinate vectors, this row-by-column multiplication corresponds to the usual dot product when the row contains the components of the first vector. However, for complex vectors, we must be more careful: the standard Hermitian inner product involves complex conjugation, usually written using the conjugate transpose \(\mathbf a^*\), rather than simply the transpose \(\mathbf a^T\). Thus, a general linear functional and an inner product should not be treated as the same thing.

Row vectors representing linear functionals are also known as covectors. In differential geometry, covectors play an important role: a smooth field of covectors is called a differential 1-form or simply a one-form. We will return to these ideas when we study dual spaces and differential geometry.

Vectors in 2-Dimension

A vector with two components \( v_1 \) and \( v_2 \) corresponds to a point in the \(xy\)-plane, and the vector can be represented as the directed line from the origin \((0,0)\) to the point \( (v_1, v_2) \). (Note: vectors themselves do not have to start at the origin — any vector can be translated — but a position vector is usually drawn from the origin.) An example of a 2-Dimensional vector is shown below:

Vector addition is an operation where if \(\vec{v} = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\vec{w} = \begin{pmatrix} w_1 \\ w_2 \end{pmatrix}\), then vector addition is: \[ \vec{v} + \vec{w} = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} w_1 \\ w_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 + w_1 \\ v_2 + w_2 \end{pmatrix} \] Let the vectors be \(\vec{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\vec{w} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\). Then the addition of these two vectors is: \[ \vec{v} + \vec{w} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} \] Below shows what it geometrically looks like when we add these two vectors:

Geometrically, when we add two vectors what basically happens is that we translate either of the vectors so its tail coincides with the head of the other vector. The resultant vector goes from the tail of the first to the head of the translated second vector.

Scalar Multiplication

Scalar multiplication is a vector operation: if we have a vector \(\vec{v} = \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix}\) then scalar multiplication is the operation \(c\vec{v}\) where \(c\) is a scalar such that: \[ c\vec{v} = c \cdot \begin{pmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} cv_1 \\ cv_2 \end{pmatrix} \] What scalar multiplication does is it scales the vector's components by an amount \(c\) and hence the whole vector gets scaled by a factor of \(c\). Below in the animation we can see what happens to a vector when it is being scaled by a scalar \(c\):

When \(c>0\) the vector gets scaled by a factor of \(c\), and when \(c \lt 0\) the vector gets scaled by a factor of \(|c|\) and also gets flipped in the opposite direction. And when \(c=0\) the vector gets scaled to the zero vector. All three scenarios are shown in the above animation.

Linear Combination

Given some vectors \(\vec{v}_1,\vec{v}_2,\dots,\vec{v}_n\), their linear combination is defined as: \[ L = c_1 \vec{v}_1 + c_2 \vec{v}_2 + \dots + c_n \vec{v}_n \] Where \(c_i \in \mathbb{F}\) (the \(c\)'s could be real or complex).

We can show an example. Let there be three vectors \(\vec{v}_1= \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4\end{pmatrix}\), \(\vec{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\vec{v}_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\), and let the scalars be \(c_1 = 0.5\), \(c_2 = 2\) and \(c_3 = 1.5\). So their linear combination is: \[ c_1 \vec{v}_1 + c_2 \vec{v}_2 + c_3 \vec{v}_3 = 0.5\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4\end{pmatrix} + 2\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2\end{pmatrix} + 1.5\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 10 \\ 9\end{pmatrix}. \] (Note: the third term uses \(\vec{v}_3=\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\end{pmatrix}\), not \(\vec{v}_2\).)

Above we can see how a linear combination for three vectors looks. There is another important concept about linear combination: span.

Vectors in 3-Dimension

A vector in 3-dimensional space is a vector with three components \(v_1\), \(v_2\) and \(v_3\). Vectors in 3-dimensions are arrows with a starting point and an endpoint in xyz space.

Vector Space

Vector space is a set of objects called vectors, such that certain axioms are obeyed by the vectors under the addition and scalar multiplication operators defined over the vector space.

A particular vector space is the set \(\mathbb F^n\); this is the set of tuples \(\mathbb{V} = \{(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)\mid x_i\in \mathbb{F}\}\) for some field \(\mathbb{F}\) such as \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\). The elements of a vector space \(\mathbb F^n\) are called points or vectors and obey the axioms of vector space as shown below in the definition. The vector space \(\mathbb F^n\) is the finite-dimensional vector space over the real numbers in the case we write it as \(\mathbb R^n\), otherwise if it is the finite-dimensional vector space over the complex numbers \(\mathbb{C}\) we write it as \(\mathbb C^n\), with dimension \(n\).

Such a set, let's call it \(\mathbb{V}\), that obeys the axioms of vector space as stated below is called a vector space. And we have seen an example of a vector space, that is the vector space \(\mathbb{F}^n\) above. The elements of a vector space are called vectors, and the set of all vectors in a vector space is called the vector space itself.

Consider \(\mathbb V = \mathbb{F}^n\), \(n=2\), and \(\mathbb F = \mathbb{C}\), then \(\mathbb V = \mathbb{C}^2\) is the set of all ordered pairs of complex numbers. The elements of \(\mathbb{C}^2\) are called vectors, and they can be represented as column vectors: \[ \mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} a+b\mathit{i} \\ c+d\mathit{i} \end{pmatrix}, \quad a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{R} \]

Note: \(\mathbb{F}\) stands for a field, usually \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\).

DEFINITION: Vector Space A vector space over a field \(\mathbb{F}\) is a set \(\mathbb{V}\) equipped with two operations: vector addition and scalar multiplication. These operations are defined as follows:

\[ + : \mathbb{V} \times \mathbb{V} \longrightarrow \mathbb{V} \]

\[ \cdot : \mathbb{F} \times \mathbb{V} \longrightarrow \mathbb{V} \]

The first operation assigns a vector in \(\mathbb{V}\) to every pair of vectors in \(\mathbb{V}\), while the second assigns a vector in \(\mathbb{V}\) to every scalar-vector pair. We denote these operations by \(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}\) and \(a\mathbf{v}\), respectively. They must satisfy the following properties:

Commutativity:

\[ \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{w} + \mathbf{v} \quad \text{for all} \quad \mathbf{v},\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{V}. \]

Associativity of Vector Addition:

\[ (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}) + \mathbf{u} = \mathbf{v} + (\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{u}) \quad \text{for all} \quad \mathbf{v},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{V}. \]

Compatibility of Scalar Multiplication:

\[ (ab)\mathbf{v} = a(b\mathbf{v}) \quad \text{for all} \quad a,b \in \mathbb{F},\ \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{V}. \]

Additive Identity:

There exists an element \(\mathbf{0} \in \mathbb{V}\) such that \[ \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v} \quad \text{for all} \quad \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{V}. \] We call \(\mathbf{0}\) the zero vector.

Additive Inverse:

For every \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{V}\), there exists \(\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{V}\) such that \[ \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{0}. \]

Scalar Identity:

\[ 1\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} \quad \text{for all} \quad \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{V}, \] where \(1\) is the multiplicative identity of the field \(\mathbb{F}\).

Distributive Properties:

\[ a(\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}) = a\mathbf{v} + a\mathbf{w} \] and \[ (a+b)\mathbf{v} = a\mathbf{v} + b\mathbf{v}, \] for all \(a,b \in \mathbb{F}\) and \(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w} \in \mathbb{V}\).

Keep this in mind even though throughout many lesson we will moslty talk about \(\mathbb{V} = \mathbb{F}^n\) \(\mathbb F^n\) is not only vector space. There are vector spaces of all continuous real-valued (or complex-valued) functions defined on the closed, bounded interval \([a,b]\) donoted as \(C[a,b]\), there is also a sequence space consisting of all real or complex sequence \(x = (x_n) \) whose \(p\)-th powers have a finite sum. For \(1 \leq p \lt \infty\) it is defined by: \[ ||x||_p = \left( \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |x_n|^p \right)^{1/p} \lt \infty \] and for \(p = \infty\) it is defined by: \[ ||x||_\infty = \sup_{n \in \mathbb{N}} |x_n| \] There are many such example of abstract vector spaces, and we will discuss them in later chapter of funcational analysis. For now, we will focus on the vector space \(\mathbb{F}^n\).

Now more casually speaking: the space \(\mathbb{R}^n\) consists of all column vectors \(\mathbf{v}\) with \(n\) real components, and the space \(\mathbb{C}^n\) consists of all column vectors \(\mathbf{v}\) with \(n\) complex components. Note: Throughout our lesson when we speak of a vector \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{F}^n\) we mean \(\mathbf{v}\) is a column vector rather than a row vector. A space of row vectors is the coordinate model of the dual space once a basis is fixed. Until we cover dual spaces later in this chapter, we will treat vectors as column vectors unless we explicitly say a vector is a row vector.

We can give some examples of \(\mathbb{R}^n\), such as \(\mathbb{R}^2\), \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and \(\mathbb{R}^5\). The set \(\mathbb{R}^2\) can be thought of as a plane — it is the set of all ordered pairs of real numbers: \[ \mathbb{R}^2 = \{(x,y) : x,y \in \mathbb{R}\}. \] Examples of elements of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) are \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 3\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{w} = \begin{pmatrix}6 \\ 9\end{pmatrix}\). Similarly, for \(\mathbb{R}^3\): \[ \mathbb{R}^3 = \{(x,y,z) : x,y,z \in \mathbb{R}\}. \] Examples of elements of \(\mathbb{R}^3\) are \(\mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix}2 \\ 3 \\ 9\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix}6 \\ 9 \\ 2\end{pmatrix}\). In general, \[ \mathbb{R}^n = \{(v_1,v_2,\dots,v_n) : v_i \in \mathbb{R}\ \text{for } i=1,\dots,n\}, \] and \[ \mathbb{C}^n = \{(v_1,v_2,\dots,v_n) : v_i \in \mathbb{C}\ \text{for } i=1,\dots,n\}. \]

Proof: Unique Additive Identity

We stated the additive identity axiom as \(\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{v}\). One natural question is: what is this \(\mathbf{0}\), and how many such vectors are there in a vector space? Intuitively we call it the zero vector and expect it to be unique. That intuition can be turned into a short, rigorous proof using only the axiom of additive identity.

Suppose \(\mathbf{0}_0\) and \(\mathbf{0}_1\) are both additive identities in a vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). Then, using the fact that \(\mathbf{0}_0\) is an identity for every vector and \(\mathbf{0}_1\) is an identity for every vector, we have \[ \mathbf{0}_1 = \mathbf{0}_1 + \mathbf{0}_0 \quad\text{(since \(\mathbf{0}_0\) is an identity)} \] and also \[ \mathbf{0}_1 + \mathbf{0}_0 = \mathbf{0}_0 \quad\text{(since \(\mathbf{0}_1\) is an identity)}. \] Combining these equalities gives \(\mathbf{0}_1 = \mathbf{0}_0\). Hence the additive identity is unique.

Proof: Unique Additive Inverse

Every element of a vector space has a unique additive inverse.

Let \(\mathbb{V}\) be a vector space and fix \(\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{V}\). Suppose \(\mathbf{w}_0\) and \(\mathbf{w}_1\) are both additive inverses of \(\mathbf{v}\); that is, \[ \mathbf{w}_0 + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \quad\text{and}\quad \mathbf{w}_1 + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}, \] where \(\mathbf{0}\) is the (unique) additive identity. Then \[ \mathbf{w}_0 = \mathbf{w}_0 + \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{w}_0 + (\mathbf{w}_1 + \mathbf{v}) = (\mathbf{w}_0 + \mathbf{w}_1) + \mathbf{v} = (\mathbf{w}_0 + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w}_1 = \mathbf{0} + \mathbf{w}_1 = \mathbf{w}_1, \] where we used associativity and the defining equalities of \(\mathbf{w}_0\) and \(\mathbf{w}_1\). Thus \(\mathbf{w}_0 = \mathbf{w}_1\), so the additive inverse of \(\mathbf{v}\) is unique.

Linear Independence and Span

We have shown that a linear combination is an operation on vectors that belong to a vector space. The vectors are scaled by scalars and added up, giving us a new vector that also belongs to the vector space. \[ \mathbf{L} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n \] where \(\mathbf{v}_i, \mathbf{L} \in \mathbb{V}\) and \(c_i \in \mathbb{F}\).

Below in the animation, we can see a linear combination of three vectors and how the resultant vector changes as we change and try different values of \(c_1,c_2,c_3\). We can see that as we change the values of the coefficients, the magnitude and direction of the resultant vector change, and its endpoint moves around the \(xy\)-plane. But does this happen for every set of vectors? Are there vectors we can linearly combine and get a resultant vector that is restricted to certain regions or points? That is what we are going to explore now.

Linear Independence and Dependence

Given a linear combination: \[ \mathbf{L} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n \] the vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n \in \mathbb{V}\) are said to be linearly independent if the only way for \(\mathbf{L}\) to be zero is by making all \(c_i\)'s zero. Otherwise, the vectors are said to be linearly dependent.

To understand it better, let's look at the animation below:

Here we can see that by changing the values of the constants, our resultant vector has the potential to reach every point on the \(xy\)-plane. Our specific choice for the vectors was \(\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\). In this particular case, since we have two vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), the fact that their linear combinations can reach every point on the plane tells us that they are linearly independent. This does not happen for every linearly independent set of vectors, as we will see when we look at vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

If the vectors were not linearly independent, we could write their linear combination as: \[ \mathbf{0} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n \] where at least one of the coefficients is nonzero. Suppose \(c_i \ne 0\). We can then write: \[ \mathbf{v}_i = -\sum_{\substack{j=1\\j\ne i}}^n \frac{c_j}{c_i}\mathbf{v}_j \] Since \(c_i\) is not zero, we can divide by it and express \(\mathbf{v}_i\) as a linear combination of the other vectors. This means that at least one vector can be written in terms of the others, and hence the vectors are linearly dependent.

Here above in the animation we can see that the two vectors are scaled by different amounts and lie along the same line through the origin, but point in opposite directions. The angle between the two scaled vectors is \(180^\circ\). For our specific choice of vectors, we have: \[ \mathbf{0} = 1 \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} - 2 \cdot \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \] Thus we call them linearly dependent. We can also see that by changing the values of the constants we can reach every point that lies on the straight line that passes through the origin and has the slope \(4/2=2\), or \(y=2x\). In the \(xy\)-plane, two linearly dependent vectors always produce a vector via linear combination that is restricted to the same line through the origin, provided at least one of the vectors is nonzero.

Span

Span is the set of all linear combinations of a list of vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\) in vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). The set is denoted by \(\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n)\). \[ \mathrm{span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n) = \left\{ c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n : c_1, \dots, c_n \in \mathbb{F} \right\} \]

We will go back to our previous two animations:



Notice that in the first animation, the two vectors are linearly independent. We saw previously one case where, because the vectors were dependent, the resultant vector was strictly restricted to a line. But here the resultant vector is not restricted to a line. Since we have two linearly independent vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), their linear combinations can reach every possible point on the plane. In the second animation, we can also see how the resultant vector can reach every point on the plane. However, having three vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) does not mean they are linearly independent. Any three vectors in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) are linearly dependent, even though they can still span the whole plane.

In the case of vectors in 3D space, it is a little different. Below in the animation we see two vectors in 3D space. By changing the values of the constants the resultant vector moves around, accessing different points:

Notice that our resultant vector is restricted to a plane. Look carefully below, we have shown the plane:

This is because we have two linearly independent vectors in 3D space. The plane they span does not have to be the \(xy\)-plane; it depends on the two vectors. But how do we reach points outside this plane? Well, we add a third vector that is not in the plane spanned by the first two vectors. In other words, it cannot be written as a linear combination of those two vectors. This third vector gives us a direction outside the plane, allowing our resultant vector to access all points in 3D space. Below we show that via the animation:

Going back to Span, span is the set of all vectors that are reachable using linear combinations of vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n \in \mathbb{V}\). And it is denoted by \(\mathrm{span}(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n)\).

If the span of a set of vectors is a line through the origin and reaches every point on that line, then we say: "The vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n \in \mathbb{V}\) span that line." A line through the origin is a one-dimensional subspace, which is isomorphic to \(\mathbb{F}^1\). If the vectors' linear combinations reach every point on a plane through the origin, then we say the vectors span that plane. Such a plane is a two-dimensional subspace, which is isomorphic to \(\mathbb{F}^2\). And if the vectors span the whole space, then we say they span the whole \(\mathbb{F}^m\), when the vectors belong to \(\mathbb{F}^m\).

Subspace

We know what a vector space is. A subspace is a subset of the vector space that is also a vector space. Note that subset does not necessarily mean it is also a vector space. If it is a subset of a vector space and does not follow the axioms of a vector space, then all it is is just a subset. More formally:

DEFINITION: Subspace A subset \(\mathbb{U}\) of \(\mathbb{V}\) is called a subspace if \(\mathbb{U}\) also obeys the axioms of a vector space.

Additive Identity:

\(\mathbf{0} \in \mathbb U\)

Closed Under Addition:

\(u,w \in \mathbb U \implies u+w \in \mathbb U\)

Closed Under Scalar Multiplication:

\(a \in \mathbb F\) and \(u \in \mathbb U \implies au \in \mathbb U\)

Example: \(\{(x_1, x_2,0): x_1,x_2 \in \mathbb{F}\}\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb{F}^3\). It is like having a 3D space and a plane that passes through the origin. Indeed, a plane passing through the origin of a 3D space is a vector space. It is a \(\mathbb{F}^2\) vector space and is a subspace of \(\mathbb{F}^3\).

Let's look at a few simple examples:

A line passing through the origin is a \(\mathbb{R}^1\) subspace of a vector space. Lines can be defined by a linear equation with no displacement so that it must pass through the origin.

A plane that passes through the origin is a one dimensional subspace of a vector space. The plane can be defined by an equation of the form \(c_1x+c_2y+c_3z = 0\).

The reason they must pass through the origin is that a subspace is also a vector space, and a vector space must have an origin. Alright, but given the vectors how do we find the space that they span?

EXAMPLE

Given the linearly independent vectors \( \mathbf{v_1} = \begin{pmatrix} 2.2 \\ 0.8 \\ 0.5 \end{pmatrix}\) and \( \mathbf{v_2} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.8 \\ 2.0 \\ 1.1 \end{pmatrix}\) what is the plane that they span? Find \(\text{span}(\mathbf{v_1}, \mathbf{v_2})\).



We have the vectors \( \mathbf{v_1} = \begin{pmatrix} 2.2 \\ 0.8 \\ 0.5 \end{pmatrix}\) and \( \mathbf{v_2} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.8 \\ 2.0 \\ 1.1 \end{pmatrix}\). The plane they span is the plane they lie on. It is the same reason why two-dimensional vectors that are linearly independent on the \(xy\) plane span the \(xy\) plane.

Here is the thing. Now any vector, assume \(\mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix}\), is a vector that lies on the plane \(\text{span}(\mathbf{v_1}, \mathbf{v_2})\). Now assume another vector called \(\mathbf{n}\) that is perpendicular to the plane and so is perpendicular to every vector on the plane.

Now that we have a vector \(\mathbf{n}\) perpendicular to the plane, and hence perpendicular to \(\mathbf{X}\), we can use the fact that the dot product between two perpendicular vectors is zero. But for that we first need to know the perpendicular vector \(\mathbf{n}\). To find \(\mathbf{n}\) we just need to take the cross product of \(\mathbf{v_1}\) and \(\mathbf{v_2}\). Then we get: \[ \mathbf{n} = \begin{pmatrix} 2.2 \\ 0.8 \\ 0.5 \end{pmatrix} \times \begin{pmatrix} -0.8 \\ 2.0 \\ 1.1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -0.12 \\ -2.82 \\ 5.04 \end{pmatrix} \]

Now taking the dot product we get: \[ \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{X} = 0 \] \[ \Rightarrow \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{X} = 0 = \begin{pmatrix} -0.12 \\ -2.82 \\ 5.04 \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix} \] Taking the dot product, we get: \[ \Rightarrow -0.12x - 2.82y + 5.04z = 0 \] and this is the equation of the plane our vectors \(\mathbf{v_1}\) and \(\mathbf{v_2}\) span, and hence the animation below shows it:

So span is basically all points that a linear combination of some vectors gives.

Column space

Column space is a specific type of subspace. Given a matrix \(\mathbf{A}\), the column space is the subspace spanned by the column vectors of the matrix.

Given an example, assume we have the matrix: \[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \] The column space of this matrix \(\mathbf{A}\) is denoted by \(Ra(A)\) and is: \[ Ra(A) = x \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ 0\\ 2 \end{pmatrix} + y \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + z \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} \] Column space is a special type of subspace and in upcoming lessons we will see it used often. There are also row space, null space, left null space, etc.
