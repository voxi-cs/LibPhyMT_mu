In this lesson we will explore some abstract but intuitive concepts. These are useful ideas what will be needed in analysis. They are intuitive ideas that in finite dimentional vector space.

Bases

Given a finite-dimensional vector space, every vector in the vector space can be expressed as a linear combination of certain vectors in that vector space. These vectors are what we call a basis. Bases are quite important when working with vectors, as they allow us to represent vectors using coordinates and take advantage of geometry and analysis to solve many important and beautiful problems in engineering, physics, and mathematics.

Definition: basis A basis of a vector space \(\mathbb{V}\) is a list of vectors in \(\mathbb{V}\) that is linearly independent and spans the vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). In a finite-dimensional vector space, a basis has the minimum possible number of vectors among all spanning lists.

Assume we have a vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). It has many points/vectors. Now assume we have a collection of linearly independent vectors that we can linearly combine to get every vector in the vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). That is when we call this collection of vectors a basis of the vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). The elements of a basis are called basis vectors.

In other words, a basis is a set of linearly independent vectors that is also a spanning set of \(\mathbb{V}\).

Some familiar basis vectors \(\mathbf{\hat{i}}\), \(\mathbf{\hat{j}}\), and \(\mathbf{\hat{k}}\)

We are familiar with the basis vectors \(\mathbf{\hat{i}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{\hat{j}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\), and \(\mathbf{\hat{k}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\). These are the standard basis vectors of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). You may also know that we used to write vectors such as \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}\) as \(\mathbf{v} = 4\mathbf{\hat{i}} + 2\mathbf{\hat{j}} + 6\mathbf{\hat{k}}\). Notice that this is just a linear combination: \[ \mathbf{v} = 4\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + 2\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + 6\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \] These basis vectors allow us to reach every vector in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), which is why they are called basis vectors. The specific reason we use them to represent vectors in 3D is that each component can be read directly from the coefficients, so no extra calculation is needed. A vector space can have many different bases.

A vector space can have many bases. The list of vectors \(\left(\left(1,0,\dots,0\right), \left(0,1,\dots,0\right), \dots, \left(0,0,\dots,1\right)\right)\) is a basis of \(\mathbf{F}^n\). This basis is a special type of basis, so we call it the standard basis of \(\mathbf{F}^n\).

As the vectors \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\), and \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\) span \(\mathbb{R}^3\), they form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). The vectors \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\), and \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\) also form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). However, \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\) do not form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and neither do \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ -4 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ -5 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}\), because two vectors cannot span all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Criterion for basis

A collection of vectors of a vector space \(\mathbb{V}\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{V}\) if and only if every vector \(\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{V}\) can be written uniquely in the form: \[ \mathbf{v} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1+c_2\mathbf{v}_2+\dots+c_n\mathbf{v}_n \] where \(c_1,c_2,\dots,c_n \in \mathbb{F}\).

What it means is that if \( \mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n \in \mathbb{V} \) are basis vectors of the vector space \(\mathbb{V}\), then every unique/different choice of \(c_1,c_2,\dots,c_n\) will give different vectors, and the linear combination of the vectors gives all vectors of the vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). Below is the proof:

Theorem-3.001:

If \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\) are basis vectors of a vector space \(\mathbb{V}\), then every vector \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{V}\) can be written as a unique linear combination of these basis vectors. That is, \[ \mathbf{v} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n \] for one and only one set of coefficients \(c_1,c_2,\dots,c_n\).

Proof:

Let \(\mathbf{v}\) be a vector in the vector space \(\mathbb{V}\), so \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{V}\).

Suppose \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\) are basis vectors of \(\mathbb{V}\), and hence they must be linearly independent.

Assume the vector \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{V}\) can be written in two different ways using a linear combination of the basis vectors with different sets of coefficients, let them be \(c\)'s and \(a\)'s. Hence: \[ \mathbf{v} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n \] \[ \mathbf{v} = a_1\mathbf{v}_1 + a_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + a_n\mathbf{v}_n \] Now if we subtract them, we get: \[ \mathbf{0} = (c_1-a_1)\mathbf{v}_1 + (c_2-a_2)\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + (c_n-a_n)\mathbf{v}_n \] Since the vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\) are linearly independent, the only way for the linear combination above to be zero is if and only if \(c_i-a_i = 0\) for every \(i\). And hence: \[ \boxed{c_i = a_i} \] So it contradicts our assumption of "different sets of coefficients". Hence, every vector in \(\mathbb{V}\) has a unique set of coefficients when written as a linear combination of the basis vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\).

NOTE: Go back to the definition and remember we said list of minimum number of vectors. If you ignore that, then every linearly independent list becomes a basis, and that is not correct. For \(\mathbb{R}^1\) the number is one, for \(\mathbb{R}^2\) it is two, for \(\mathbb{R}^3\) it is three, and for \(\mathbb{R}^n\) the number is \(n\). This is what we call a dimension.

Dimension

We many times mentioned the word "dimension" without defining it first. It is my bad, but this is a good time to define it. Dimension is quite an easy idea, but quite an intriguing one. Soon we will move to vector spaces with infinite dimension rather than studying finite-dimensional vector spaces, but right now we will look at finite-dimensional vector spaces.

Definition: Dimension Dimension of a vector space is the minimum number of vectors needed to form a basis of a vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). That is, for a vector space \(\mathbf{F}^n\), the dimension is \(n\).

Alright this is how we define dimension. One question arises: why do we assume that all bases of a vector space have the same length? Below we will prove it, but before that we need to two more statments.

Lemma-3.0.1: Linear Dependence Lemma

Suppose \(v_1,v_2,\dots,v_m\) are linearly dependent vectors in \(\mathbb V\). Then there exists \(j \in \{1,2,\dots,m\}\) such that:

\((i):\) \(v_j \in \text{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1})\).

\((ii):\) If \(v_j\) from \((i)\) is removed, meaning the \(j^{\text{th}}\) term is removed from the list \(v_1,\dots,v_m\), then the span of the remaining vectors is the same as the span of \(v_1,\dots,v_m\).

Proof:

\((i):\) Since \(v_1,\dots,v_m\) are a linearly dependent list of vectors in \(\mathbb V\), there exist \(a_1,\dots,a_m \in \mathbb F\), not all zero, such that: \[ a_1v_1+\dots+a_mv_m=0. \] Choose the largest \(j\in\{1,\dots,m\}\) such that \(a_j\neq0\). Because \(j\) is the largest such index, we have \(a_{j+1}=\dots=a_m=0\). Therefore: \[ a_1v_1+\dots+a_jv_j=0. \] Since \(a_j\neq0\), we can solve for \(v_j\): \[ v_j=-\frac{a_1}{a_j}v_1+\dots-\frac{a_{j-1}}{a_j}v_{j-1}. \] Therefore: \[ v_j\in\text{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1}). \]

\((ii):\) Let \(u\in\text{span}(v_1,\dots,v_m)\). Then \(u\) can be written as: \[ u=b_1v_1+\dots+b_{j-1}v_{j-1}+b_jv_j+b_{j+1}v_{j+1}+\dots+b_mv_m \] for some \(b_1,\dots,b_m\in\mathbb F\). From \((i)\), we know that: \[ v_j=-\frac{a_1}{a_j}v_1+\dots-\frac{a_{j-1}}{a_j}v_{j-1}. \] So we can substitute this expression for \(v_j\) into the expression for \(u\). After substitution, \(u\) is written entirely in terms of the remaining vectors: \[ u\in\text{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1},v_{j+1},\dots,v_m). \] Since \(u\) was arbitrary: \[ \text{span}(v_1,\dots,v_m) \subseteq \text{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1},v_{j+1},\dots,v_m). \] On the other hand, every remaining vector \(v_i\), where \(i\neq j\), is already one of \(v_1,\dots,v_m\). Therefore: \[ \text{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1},v_{j+1},\dots,v_m) \subseteq \text{span}(v_1,\dots,v_m). \] Thus, both inclusions hold, and therefore: \[ \boxed{ \text{span}(v_1,\dots,v_m) = \text{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1},v_{j+1},\dots,v_m) } \]

Theorem-3.002: Length of a linearly independent list \(\le\) Length of a spanning list.

Let \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) be a linearly independent list of vectors in a vector space \(\mathbb V\), and let \(w_1,\dots,w_n\) be a spanning list of vectors in \(\mathbb V\). Then the length of the linearly independent list cannot be greater than the length of the spanning list. In other words, \[ \boxed{m\le n}. \]

Proof:

Suppose \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) is a linearly independent list of vectors in \(\mathbb V\), and \(w_1,\dots,w_n\) is a spanning list of vectors in \(\mathbb V\). We want to prove that: \[ m\le n. \] Here is the idea: we start with the spanning list \(w_1,\dots,w_n\), and then add the \(u\)'s to it one at a time. Every time we add a \(u\), we remove one of the \(w\)'s so that the list keeps spanning \(\mathbb V\). (Note: This proof was adopted from Axler's LDR.)

Step 1: Start with: \[ \mathbf B=(w_1,\dots,w_n). \] Since \(\mathbf B\) spans \(\mathbb V\), we know that \(u_1\) can be written as a linear combination of the \(w\)'s. Therefore, the list: \( u_1,w_1,\dots,w_n \) is linearly dependent. By the Linear Dependence Lemma, we can remove one vector from this list without changing its span. We cannot remove \(u_1\), because \(u_1\) by itself is linearly independent. Therefore, one of the \(w\)'s can be removed. So now we have a list \(\mathbf B\) of length \(n\), containing \(u_1\), which still spans \(\mathbb V\).

Step \(j\): Now suppose that we already have a list \(\mathbf B\) of length \(n\) that spans \(\mathbb V\), and it contains: \[ u_1,\dots,u_{j-1}. \] Add \(u_j\) to this list. Since \(\mathbf B\) already spans \(\mathbb V\), the new list becomes linearly dependent. So, by the Linear Dependence Lemma, we can remove one vector while keeping the same span. But we cannot remove any of: \[ u_1,\dots,u_j, \] because these vectors are linearly independent. Therefore, the vector we remove must be one of the \(w\)'s. After removing it, we again have a list of length \(n\) that spans \(\mathbb V\), but now contains: \[ u_1,\dots,u_j. \]

We can repeat this process until all \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) have been added. Each time we add one \(u\), we must remove one \(w\). Therefore, we need at least as many \(w\)'s as there are \(u\)'s. Hence: \[ \boxed{m\le n}. \] Therefore: \[ \text{Length of a linearly independent list} \le \text{Length of a spanning list}. \]

Theorem-3.003: Length of all bases of a vector space is equal.

Let \(\mathbb V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space. Then every basis of \(\mathbb V\) has the same number of vectors. In other words, if \(\mathbf B_1\) and \(\mathbf B_2\) are two bases of \(\mathbb V\), then: \[ \boxed{\text{Length}(\mathbf B_1)=\text{Length}(\mathbf B_2)}. \]

Proof:

We have just seen: \[ \text{Length of a linearly independent list} \le \text{Length of a spanning list}. \] Now assume we have two lists of basis vectors of some vector space \(\mathbb V\), and they are \(\mathbf{B}_1\) and \(\mathbf{B}_2\). Here is the thing: these two lists are both linearly independent and spanning lists. Therefore, using the result from the previous theorem: \[ \text{Length}(\mathbf{B}_1) \le \text{Length}(\mathbf{B}_2). \] Similarly: \[ \text{Length}(\mathbf{B}_2) \le \text{Length}(\mathbf{B}_1). \] The only way both inequalities can hold is if: \[ \boxed{\text{Length}(\mathbf{B}_1)=\text{Length}(\mathbf{B}_2)}. \] Therefore, every basis of a finite-dimensional vector space has the same length.

Now we can say more properly, that the definition of Dimension is:

Definition: Dimension Dimension of a finite-dimensional vector space is the length of any basis of the vector space.

We denote the dimension of a vector space \(\mathbb{V}\) as \(\dim \mathbb{V}\).

Another proof I think we should explore is that the dimension of a subspace is always going to be less than the dimension of the vector space that it is a subspace of. For example, a plane is a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and indeed the plane has lower dimension than the whole 3D space. This is because \(\dim \mathbb{R}^{2}\) is \(2\) and \(\dim \mathbb{R}^{3}\) is \(3\). But we cannot rely on a simple example; we need a proof for the general case.

Theorem-3.004: \(\dim \mathbb{U} \le \dim \mathbb{V}\).

Let \(\mathbb{U}\) be a subspace of a finite-dimensional vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). Then the dimension of \(\mathbb{U}\) cannot be greater than the dimension of \(\mathbb{V}\). That is: \[ \boxed{\dim \mathbb{U} \le \dim \mathbb{V}}. \]

Proof:

Assume a vector space \(\mathbb{V}\) and a subspace of it \(\mathbb{U}\). Now see, basis vectors are lists of spanning vectors and lists of linearly independent vectors of a vector space. The basis of \(\mathbb{V}\) is both a list of spanning vectors and a list of linearly independent vectors, but notice the basis of \(\mathbb{U}\) is also a list of linearly independent vectors and a spanning list for \(\mathbb{U}\), even though it does not span \(\mathbb{V}\). So using our previous theorem: \[ \text{Length of a linearly independent list} \le \text{Length of a spanning list} \] Assume the basis of \(\mathbb{U}\) is \(\mathbf{B}_U\) and the basis of \(\mathbb{V}\) is \(\mathbf{B}_V\), so \[ \text{Length}(\mathbf{B}_U) \le \text{Length}(\mathbf{B}_V). \] Hence: \[ \boxed{\dim \mathbb{U} \le \dim \mathbb{V}} \]

Definition: Sum of subspace \(\mathbb U_1 + \mathbb U_2\). Let \(\mathbb U_1\) and \(\mathbb U_2\) be two subspaces of a vector space \(\mathbb V\). The sum of \(\mathbb U_1\) and \(\mathbb U_2\), denoted by \(\mathbb U_1 + \mathbb U_2\), is the set of all vectors that can be written as the sum of one vector from \(\mathbb U_1\) and one vector from \(\mathbb U_2\). That is,

\[ \mathbb U_1 + \mathbb U_2 = \left\{ \mathbf{u}_1+\mathbf{u}_2 \mid \mathbf{u}_1\in\mathbb U_1,\; \mathbf{u}_2\in\mathbb U_2 \right\}. \]

The sum \(\mathbb U_1+\mathbb U_2\) is itself a subspace of \(\mathbb V\).

Theorem-3.005: \(\mathbb{U}_1 + \mathbb{U}_2\) is a subspace.

Suppose \(\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(\mathbb{U}_2\) are two subspaces of a vector space \(\mathbb V\). Then their sum \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\), defined by \[ \mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2 = \{u_1+u_2\mid u_1\in\mathbb{U}_1,\;u_2\in\mathbb{U}_2\}, \] is also a subspace of \(\mathbb V\).

Proof:

In order to prove that \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\) is a subspace, we have to show that it contains the zero vector and is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication. First, since \(\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(\mathbb{U}_2\) are subspaces, we know that \(0\in\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(0\in\mathbb{U}_2\). Therefore: \[ 0=0+0\in\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2. \] Now let \(x,y\in\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\). Then by the definition of the sum, there exist \(u_1,v_1\in\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(u_2,v_2\in\mathbb{U}_2\) such that: \[ x=u_1+u_2,\qquad y=v_1+v_2. \] Therefore: \[ x+y=(u_1+u_2)+(v_1+v_2) =(u_1+v_1)+(u_2+v_2). \] Since \(\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(\mathbb{U}_2\) are subspaces, we have \(u_1+v_1\in\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(u_2+v_2\in\mathbb{U}_2\). Hence: \[ x+y\in\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2. \] So \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\) is closed under vector addition. Finally, let \(x\in\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\) and \(c\) be any scalar. Then there exist \(u_1\in\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(u_2\in\mathbb{U}_2\) such that \(x=u_1+u_2\). Therefore: \[ cx=c(u_1+u_2)=cu_1+cu_2. \] Since \(\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(\mathbb{U}_2\) are subspaces, we have \(cu_1\in\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(cu_2\in\mathbb{U}_2\). Thus: \[ cx\in\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2. \] Therefore \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\) contains the zero vector and is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication. Hence, by the definition of a subspace, \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb V\).

Now let us see what is the dimention of this new subspace \(\mathbb U_1 + \mathbb U_2\). Bellow is theorem that answers that, it might be little bit unintuative but we will try our best to try make sence of it.

Theorem-3.006: Dimension of a Sum

Suppose \(V\) is a finite-dimensional vector space and \(\mathbb{U}_1,\mathbb{U}_2\) are subspaces of \(V\). Then: \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2) = \dim(\mathbb{U}_1)+\dim(\mathbb{U}_2) -\dim(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2). \]

Proof:

Let \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) be a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2\). Then: \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2)=m. \] Since \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) is linearly independent in \(\mathbb{U}_1\), we can extend it to a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_1\). Let this basis be: \[ u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j. \] Therefore: \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_1)=m+j. \] Similarly, we can extend \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) to a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_2\). Let this basis be: \[ u_1,\dots,u_m,w_1,\dots,w_k. \] Therefore: \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_2)=m+k. \]

We will now show that: \[ u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j,w_1,\dots,w_k \] is a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\). First, since \(u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(u_1,\dots,u_m,w_1,\dots,w_k\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_2\), every vector in \(\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(\mathbb{U}_2\) can be written as a linear combination of the vectors above. Therefore, every vector in \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\) can also be written as a linear combination of them. Thus: \[ \mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2 = \operatorname{span}(u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j,w_1,\dots,w_k). \] So it remains only to show that these vectors are linearly independent.

Suppose: \[ a_1u_1+\cdots+a_mu_m+b_1v_1+\cdots+b_jv_j +c_1w_1+\cdots+c_kw_k=0. \] We want to show that all the \(a\)'s, \(b\)'s, and \(c\)'s are equal to \(0\). We can rewrite the equation as: \[ c_1w_1+\cdots+c_kw_k = -a_1u_1-\cdots-a_mu_m-b_1v_1-\cdots-b_jv_j. \] The left side belongs to \(\mathbb{U}_2\), since \(w_1,\dots,w_k\in\mathbb{U}_2\). Therefore the right side must also belong to \(\mathbb{U}_2\). But the right side is already a vector in \(\mathbb{U}_1\), since \(u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j\in\mathbb{U}_1\). Hence: \[ -a_1u_1-\cdots-a_mu_m-b_1v_1-\cdots-b_jv_j \in\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2. \] Since \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2\), there exist scalars \(d_1,\dots,d_m\) such that: \[ -a_1u_1-\cdots-a_mu_m-b_1v_1-\cdots-b_jv_j = d_1u_1+\cdots+d_mu_m. \] Rearranging gives: \[ (a_1+d_1)u_1+\cdots+(a_m+d_m)u_m +b_1v_1+\cdots+b_jv_j=0. \] But \(u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_1\), and therefore it is linearly independent. Thus: \[ a_i+d_i=0\qquad (i=1,\dots,m) \] and \[ b_1=\cdots=b_j=0. \] Therefore the original equation becomes: \[ c_1w_1+\cdots+c_kw_k=0. \] Since \(w_1,\dots,w_k\), together with \(u_1,\dots,u_m\), form a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_2\), they are linearly independent. Hence: \[ c_1=\cdots=c_k=0. \] Now since \(b_1=\cdots=b_j=0\) and \(c_1=\cdots=c_k=0\), the original equation reduces to: \[ a_1u_1+\cdots+a_mu_m=0. \] Again, \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) is linearly independent, so: \[ a_1=\cdots=a_m=0. \] Therefore all the coefficients are \(0\), and hence \[ u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j,w_1,\dots,w_k \] is linearly independent. Thus it is a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\), and so: \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2)=m+j+k. \] Finally, since \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_1)=m+j,\qquad \dim(\mathbb{U}_2)=m+k,\qquad \dim(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2)=m, \] we get: \[ \begin{aligned} \dim(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2) &=m+j+k\\ &=(m+j)+(m+k)-m\\ &=\dim(\mathbb{U}_1)+\dim(\mathbb{U}_2) -\dim(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2). \end{aligned} \] This proves the theorem.

To make sense of this image, consider a \(\mathbb R^3\) space, and suppose two planes are passing through the origin. They need to pass through the origin so that they are each a subspace of \(\mathbb R^3\), since they contain the origin. Then, since they both pass through the origin, two things can happen: either both planes overlap with each other, that is \(\mathbb U_1=\mathbb U_2\), or they just intersect, in which case the intersection of the planes is a line, that is \(\mathbb U_1\cap\mathbb U_2\) is a line. In this case, their sum is the whole \(\mathbb R^3\), so: \[ \mathbb U_1+\mathbb U_2=\mathbb R^3. \] Then, in the case when both planes overlap, what we get is \(\mathbb U_1+\mathbb U_2=\mathbb U_1=\mathbb U_2\), and thus \[ \dim(\mathbb U_1+\mathbb U_2) =\dim\mathbb U_1 =\dim\mathbb U_2. \] By the theorem \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2) = \dim(\mathbb{U}_1)+\dim(\mathbb{U}_2) -\dim(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2), \] here \(\mathbb U_1\cap\mathbb U_2=\mathbb U_1=\mathbb U_2\), so \[ \dim(\mathbb U_1\cap\mathbb U_2) =\dim\mathbb U_1 =\dim\mathbb U_2. \] Therefore, we get: \[ \begin{aligned} \dim(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2) &= \dim(\mathbb{U}_1)+\dim(\mathbb{U}_2) -\dim(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2)\\ &= \dim(\mathbb{U}_1)+\dim(\mathbb{U}_2)-\dim(\mathbb U_1)\\ &=\dim(\mathbb U_2), \end{aligned} \] as expected. Similarly, since \(\dim(\mathbb U_1\cap\mathbb U_2)=\dim(\mathbb U_2)\), we can also write: \[ \begin{aligned} \dim(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2) &= \dim(\mathbb{U}_1)+\dim(\mathbb{U}_2)-\dim(\mathbb U_2)\\ &=\dim(\mathbb U_1), \end{aligned} \] which is again exactly what we expected.

In the other case, when they only intersect in a line, then we get \(\mathbb U_1\cap\mathbb U_2\) is a line, so by the theorem: \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2) = 2+2-1=3. \] Here, since both are planes in \(\mathbb R^3\), that means \(\dim(\mathbb U_1)=\dim(\mathbb U_2)=2\), and since they intersect in a line, we get \(\dim(\mathbb U_1\cap\mathbb U_2)=1\). So \(\mathbb U_1+\mathbb U_2\) will span the whole space \(\mathbb R^3\). It should not be surprising, as you can imagine those two planes to be the \(xy\)-plane and the \(yz\)-plane, and as you know, linear combinations of three linearly independent vectors taken from those two planes can span the whole \(\mathbb R^3\).
