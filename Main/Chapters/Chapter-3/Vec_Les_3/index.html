In this lesson we will mainly study some of the fundamental concepts of linear algebra, which are bases and dimension. We will also prove some important theorems related to these concepts. This lesson is somewhat abstract, so in case you are not familiar with some of the fundamental concepts, you can go back to Lesson 2, where the necessary prerequisites are discussed.

Bases

Given a finite-dimensional vector space, we can find a list of vectors that allows us to express every vector in that vector space as a linear combination. These vectors must be linearly independent and span the entire vector space. Such a list of vectors is called a basis of the vector space, and its elements are called basis vectors. More precisely, a basis is defined as follows:

Definition: basis A basis of a vector space \(\mathbb{V}\) is a list of vectors in \(\mathbb{V}\) that is linearly independent and spans the vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). In a finite-dimensional vector space, a basis has the minimum possible number of vectors among all spanning lists.

Assume we have a finite-dimensional vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). Let \((v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)\) be a list of linearly independent vectors that can be linearly combined to obtain every vector in the vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). We call this list a basis of the vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). The elements of a basis are called basis vectors. As we will prove later, every basis of a finite-dimensional vector space has the same number of vectors, and this number is the minimum number of vectors needed to span the vector space.

One of the main uses of a basis is that it allows us to represent vectors of a finite-dimensional vector space using coordinates once a basis has been chosen. For example, in \(\mathbb{R}^3\), we can represent a vector as a linear combination of the standard basis vectors, as shown below.

Some familiar basis vectors \(\mathbf{\hat{i}}\), \(\mathbf{\hat{j}}\), and \(\mathbf{\hat{k}}\)

We are familiar with the basis vectors \(\mathbf{\hat{i}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{\hat{j}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\), and \(\mathbf{\hat{k}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\). These are the standard basis vectors of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). You may also know that we write vectors such as \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 2 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}\) as \(\mathbf{v} = 4\mathbf{\hat{i}} + 2\mathbf{\hat{j}} + 6\mathbf{\hat{k}}\). Notice that this is just a linear combination: \[ \mathbf{v} = 4\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + 2\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + 6\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \] These basis vectors allow us to express every vector in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) as a linear combination of them, which is why they form a basis. The specific reason we use them to represent vectors in 3D is that each component can be read directly from the coefficients, so no extra calculation is needed.

A vector space can have many different bases. The list of vectors \(\left((1,0,\dots,0), (0,1,\dots,0), \dots, (0,0,\dots,1)\right)\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{F}^n\). This basis is a special type of basis, so we call it the standard basis of \(\mathbb{F}^n\).

The vectors \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\), and \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\) form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). The vectors \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\), and \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\) also form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). However, the vectors \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\) do not form a basis of \(\mathbb{R}^3\). Neither do \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ -4 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ -5 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix}\), because two vectors cannot span all of \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Criterion for basis

A collection of vectors of a finite-dimensional vector space \(\mathbb{V}\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{V}\) if and only if every vector \(\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{V}\) can be written uniquely in the form: \[ \mathbf{v} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1+c_2\mathbf{v}_2+\dots+c_n\mathbf{v}_n \] where \(c_1,c_2,\dots,c_n \in \mathbb{F}\).

What this means is that two things must hold. First, every vector \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{V}\) must be expressible as a linear combination of the basis vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n\). This is because the basis vectors span the entire vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). Second, different choices of coefficients must give different vectors. This follows from the linear independence of the basis vectors. Together, these two properties ensure that every vector in \(\mathbb{V}\) can be written as one and only one linear combination of the basis vectors. Below is the proof:

Theorem-3.001:

If \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\) are basis vectors of a finite-dimensional vector space \(\mathbb{V}\) over a field \(\mathbb{F}\), then every vector \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{V}\) can be written as a unique linear combination of these basis vectors. That is, \[ \mathbf{v} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n \] for one and only one choice of coefficients \(c_1,c_2,\dots,c_n \in \mathbb{F}\).

Proof:

Let \(\mathbf{v}\) be any vector in the vector space \(\mathbb{V}\), so \(\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{V}\).

Suppose \(\mathbf{v}_1, \mathbf{v}_2, \dots, \mathbf{v}_n\) are basis vectors of \(\mathbb{V}\). By the definition of a basis, these vectors are linearly independent and span \(\mathbb{V}\).

Since these vectors span \(\mathbb{V}\), there must exist coefficients \(c_1,c_2,\dots,c_n \in \mathbb{F}\) such that \[ \mathbf{v} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n. \] Hence, every vector in \(\mathbb{V}\) can be expressed as a linear combination of the basis vectors.

Now we need to show that this representation is unique. Suppose the same vector \(\mathbf{v}\) can be written in two different ways as linear combinations of the basis vectors, using coefficients \(c_1,c_2,\dots,c_n\) and \(a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n\), respectively. Hence: \[ \mathbf{v} = c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + c_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + c_n\mathbf{v}_n \] \[ \mathbf{v} = a_1\mathbf{v}_1 + a_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + a_n\mathbf{v}_n \] Subtracting these two equations, we get: \[ \mathbf{0} = (c_1-a_1)\mathbf{v}_1 + (c_2-a_2)\mathbf{v}_2 + \dots + (c_n-a_n)\mathbf{v}_n. \] Since the vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n\) are linearly independent, the only way for the linear combination above to be zero is if \[ c_i-a_i=0 \] for every \(i=1,2,\dots,n\). Therefore, \[ c_i=a_i \quad \text{for every } i\in \{1,2,\dots,n\}. \] Hence, the two representations must have exactly the same coefficients. This proves that every vector in \(\mathbb{V}\) has a unique representation as a linear combination of the basis vectors \(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2,\dots,\mathbf{v}_n\).

NOTE: Go back to the definition of a basis. We defined it as a linearly independent list of vectors that spans the entire vector space. Both conditions are necessary. If we only require a list to be linearly independent, it does not necessarily become a basis because it may not span the entire vector space. For example, \(\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\) is linearly independent as a list containing one vector, but it does not span \(\mathbb{R}^2\).

For \(\mathbb{R}^1\), the standard basis contains one vector; for \(\mathbb{R}^2\), it contains two vectors; for \(\mathbb{R}^3\), it contains three vectors; and, in general, for \(\mathbb{R}^n\), the standard basis contains \(n\) vectors. Later, we will prove that all bases of a finite-dimensional vector space contain the same number of vectors. This number is what we call the dimension of the vector space.

Dimension

Given a finite-dimensional vector space \(\mathbb V\), we know that it has a finite spanning list of vectors. Suppose this list is linearly dependent. Then there is at least one vector \(v_j\) in the list that can be expressed as a linear combination of the vectors preceding it in the list. This means that we can remove \(v_j\) from the list without changing its span. We can repeat this process until we are left with a linearly independent list of vectors that still spans the vector space. Therefore, we obtain a basis of \(\mathbb V\). We will prove the result that allows us to remove such vectors below.

Lemma-3.0.1: Linear Dependence Lemma

Suppose \(\mathbb V\) is a vector space over a field \(\mathbb F\), and \(v_1,v_2,\dots,v_m\) are linearly dependent vectors in \(\mathbb V\). Then there exists \(j \in \{1,2,\dots,m\}\) such that:

\((i):\) \(v_j \in \operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1})\).

When \(j=1\), the list preceding \(v_j\) is empty. We use the convention that \(\operatorname{span}(\varnothing)=\{\mathbf 0\}\).

\((ii):\) If \(v_j\) from \((i)\) is removed from the list \(v_1,\dots,v_m\), then the span of the remaining vectors is the same as the span of \(v_1,\dots,v_m\).

Proof:

\((i):\) Since \(v_1,\dots,v_m\) are a linearly dependent list of vectors in \(\mathbb V\), there exist \(a_1,\dots,a_m \in \mathbb F\), not all zero, such that: \[ a_1v_1+\dots+a_mv_m=0. \] Choose the largest \(j\in\{1,\dots,m\}\) such that \(a_j\neq0\). Because \(j\) is the largest such index, we have \(a_{j+1}=\dots=a_m=0\). Therefore: \[ a_1v_1+\dots+a_jv_j=0. \] Since \(a_j\neq0\), we can solve for \(v_j\): \[ v_j=-\frac{a_1}{a_j}v_1+\dots-\frac{a_{j-1}}{a_j}v_{j-1}. \] Therefore: \[ v_j\in\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1}). \]

\((ii):\) Let \(u\in\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_m)\). Then \(u\) can be written as: \[ u=b_1v_1+\dots+b_{j-1}v_{j-1} +b_jv_j+b_{j+1}v_{j+1}+\dots+b_mv_m \] for some \(b_1,\dots,b_m\in\mathbb F\). From \((i)\), we know that: \[ v_j=-\frac{a_1}{a_j}v_1+\dots-\frac{a_{j-1}}{a_j}v_{j-1}. \] So we can substitute this expression for \(v_j\) into the expression for \(u\). After substitution, we get: \[ \begin{aligned} u={}& \left(b_1-\frac{b_ja_1}{a_j}\right)v_1 +\dots+ \left(b_{j-1}-\frac{b_ja_{j-1}}{a_j}\right)v_{j-1}\\ &+b_{j+1}v_{j+1}+\dots+b_mv_m. \end{aligned} \] Therefore: \[ u\in\operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1},v_{j+1},\dots,v_m). \] Since \(u\) was arbitrary: \[ \operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_m) \subseteq \operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1},v_{j+1},\dots,v_m). \] On the other hand, every remaining vector \(v_i\), where \(i\neq j\), is already one of \(v_1,\dots,v_m\). Therefore: \[ \operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1},v_{j+1},\dots,v_m) \subseteq \operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_m). \] Thus, both inclusions hold, and therefore: \[ \boxed{ \operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_m) = \operatorname{span}(v_1,\dots,v_{j-1},v_{j+1},\dots,v_m) } \]

Given a finite-dimensional vector space, can there be more linearly independent vectors in it than the number of vectors in a spanning list of that vector space? Using our intuition, we can say that the answer is no. Suppose we already have a list of \(n\) vectors that spans the entire vector space. Every vector in the vector space, including any additional linearly independent vectors, can be expressed as a linear combination of these \(n\) vectors. So we can say, we cannot have more than \(n\) linearly independent vectors. The proof below makes this precise.

Theorem-3.002: Length of a linearly independent list \(\le\) Length of a spanning list.

Let \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) be a linearly independent list of vectors in a vector space \(\mathbb V\), and let \(w_1,\dots,w_n\) be a spanning list of vectors in \(\mathbb V\). Then the length of the linearly independent list cannot be greater than the length of the spanning list. In other words: \[ \boxed{m\le n}. \]

Proof:

Suppose \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) is a linearly independent list of vectors in \(\mathbb V\), and \(w_1,\dots,w_n\) is a spanning list of vectors in \(\mathbb V\). We want to prove that: \[ m\le n. \] Here is the idea: we start with the spanning list \(w_1,\dots,w_n\), and then add the \(u\)'s to it one at a time. Every time we add a \(u\), we remove one of the remaining vectors from the spanning list so that the list keeps spanning \(\mathbb V\). We have to make sure that the \(u\)'s remain in the list throughout the process. (Note: This proof was adopted from Axler's LDR.)

Step 1: Start with: \[ \mathbf B=(w_1,\dots,w_n). \] Since \(\mathbf B\) spans \(\mathbb V\), we know that \(u_1\) can be written as a linear combination of the \(w\)'s. Therefore, the list: \[ (u_1,w_1,\dots,w_n) \] is linearly dependent. By the Linear Dependence Lemma, we can remove one vector from this list without changing its span. We cannot remove \(u_1\), because it is the first vector in the list and is linearly independent by itself, so it cannot be expressed as a linear combination of the preceding vectors. Therefore, one of the \(w\)'s can be removed. So now we have a list \(\mathbf B\) of length \(n\), containing \(u_1\), which still spans \(\mathbb V\).

Step \(j\): Now suppose that we already have a list \(\mathbf B\) of length \(n\) that spans \(\mathbb V\), and it contains: \[ u_1,\dots,u_{j-1}. \] We can write this list in the following order: \[ \mathbf B=(u_1,\dots,u_{j-1},z_1,\dots,z_{n-j+1}), \] where \(z_1,\dots,z_{n-j+1}\) are the remaining vectors in the list. Add \(u_j\) immediately after \(u_{j-1}\), before the remaining vectors. We now have: \[ (u_1,\dots,u_{j-1},u_j,z_1,\dots,z_{n-j+1}). \] Since \(\mathbf B\) spans \(\mathbb V\), the vector \(u_j\) can be written as a linear combination of the vectors in \(\mathbf B\). Therefore, the new list is linearly dependent. By the Linear Dependence Lemma, we can remove one vector while keeping the same span. But we cannot remove any of: \[ u_1,\dots,u_j, \] because these vectors are linearly independent. More precisely, none of the first \(j\) vectors can be expressed as a linear combination of the vectors preceding it. Therefore, the vector selected by the Linear Dependence Lemma must be one of the remaining \(z\)'s. After removing it, we again have a list of length \(n\) that spans \(\mathbb V\), but now contains: \[ u_1,\dots,u_j. \]

We can repeat this process, adding one \(u\) at a time and removing one of the remaining vectors from the spanning list each time. Now suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that \(m>n\). After \(n\) steps, we would have a list: \[ (u_1,\dots,u_n) \] that still spans \(\mathbb V\). Since \(m>n\), the vector \(u_{n+1}\) also exists and belongs to \(\mathbb V\). But since \(u_1,\dots,u_n\) span \(\mathbb V\), we must have: \[ u_{n+1}\in\operatorname{span}(u_1,\dots,u_n). \] This contradicts the linear independence of \(u_1,\dots,u_m\). Therefore: \[ \boxed{m\le n}. \] Hence: \[ \text{Length of a linearly independent list} \le \text{Length of a spanning list}. \]

Now we can show given any basis of a finite-dimensional vector space, all bases have the same length. This will allow us to define the dimension of a finite-dimensional vector space.

Theorem-3.003: Length of all bases of a vector space is equal.

Let \(\mathbb V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space. Then every basis of \(\mathbb V\) has the same number of vectors. In other words, if \(\mathbf B_1\) and \(\mathbf B_2\) are two bases of \(\mathbb V\), then: \[ \boxed{ \text{Length}(\mathbf B_1)=\text{Length}(\mathbf B_2) }. \]

Proof:

Since \(\mathbb V\) is finite-dimensional, it has a finite spanning list. Let the length of this list be \(n\). By the previous theorem, every finite linearly independent list in \(\mathbb V\) can have at most \(n\) vectors. Therefore, no basis of \(\mathbb V\) can have more than \(n\) vectors. In particular, every basis of \(\mathbb V\) must be finite.

We have just seen: \[ \text{Length of a linearly independent list} \le \text{Length of a spanning list}. \] Now assume we have two lists of basis vectors of some vector space \(\mathbb V\), and they are \(\mathbf B_1\) and \(\mathbf B_2\). Here is the thing: these two lists are both linearly independent and spanning lists. Therefore, using the result from the previous theorem: \[ \text{Length}(\mathbf B_1) \le \text{Length}(\mathbf B_2). \] Similarly: \[ \text{Length}(\mathbf B_2) \le \text{Length}(\mathbf B_1). \] The only way both inequalities can hold is if: \[ \boxed{ \text{Length}(\mathbf B_1)=\text{Length}(\mathbf B_2) }. \] Therefore, every basis of a finite-dimensional vector space has the same length.

Now we can now say, that the definition of Dimension is:

Definition: Dimension Dimension of a finite-dimensional vector space is the length of any basis of the vector space.

We denote the dimension of a vector space \(\mathbb V\) as \(\dim\mathbb V\).

For example, a plane passing through the origin in \(\mathbb R^3\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb R^3\). We know that two linearly independent vectors are needed to form a basis of such a plane, while three linearly independent vectors are needed to form a basis of \(\mathbb R^3\). Thus, the dimension of the plane is less than the dimension of \(\mathbb R^3\). The following theorem shows that any subspace of a finite-dimensional vector space has dimension less than or equal to the dimension of the vector space itself.

Theorem-3.004: \(\dim \mathbb{U} \le \dim \mathbb{V}\).

Let \(\mathbb{U}\) be a subspace of a finite-dimensional vector space \(\mathbb{V}\). Then the dimension of \(\mathbb{U}\) cannot be greater than the dimension of \(\mathbb{V}\). That is: \[ \boxed{\dim \mathbb{U} \le \dim \mathbb{V}}. \]

Proof:

Assume a vector space \(\mathbb{V}\) and a subspace of it \(\mathbb{U}\). Since \(\mathbb{V}\) is finite-dimensional, it has a finite basis. Let this basis be: \[ \mathbf B_V=(v_1,\dots,v_n). \] We know that \(\mathbf B_V\) is a spanning list of \(\mathbb V\). Now consider all finite linearly independent lists of vectors in \(\mathbb U\). Since \(\mathbb U\subseteq\mathbb V\), these are also linearly independent lists in \(\mathbb V\).

Using our previous theorem, the length of every such list cannot exceed \(n\), the length of the spanning list \(\mathbf B_V\). Therefore, the possible lengths of these linearly independent lists belong to the finite set: \[ \{0,1,\dots,n\}. \] So there must be a linearly independent list in \(\mathbb U\) having the maximum possible length. Let this list be \(\mathbf B_U\).

Now suppose \(\mathbf B_U\) does not span \(\mathbb U\). Then there must be some vector \(u\in\mathbb U\) such that: \[ u\notin\operatorname{span}(\mathbf B_U). \] We can add this vector to \(\mathbf B_U\), and the resulting list will still be linearly independent, since \(u\) does not belong to the span of the vectors already in the list. But this contradicts the fact that \(\mathbf B_U\) was chosen to have the maximum possible length. Therefore, \(\mathbf B_U\) must span \(\mathbb U\), and hence it is a basis of \(\mathbb U\).

Now we have a basis of both \(\mathbb U\) and \(\mathbb V\). Notice that the basis of \(\mathbb U\) is also a linearly independent list in \(\mathbb V\), even though it does not necessarily span \(\mathbb V\). So using our previous theorem: \[ \text{Length of a linearly independent list} \le \text{Length of a spanning list}. \] Therefore: \[ \text{Length}(\mathbf B_U) \le \text{Length}(\mathbf B_V). \] Hence: \[ \boxed{\dim\mathbb U\le\dim\mathbb V}. \]

Notice that equality is possible when \(\mathbb U=\mathbb V\). If \(\mathbb U\) is a proper subspace of \(\mathbb V\), then the inequality is strict. To see this, let \(u\in\mathbb V\) be a vector that does not belong to \(\mathbb U\). If we add \(u\) to a basis of \(\mathbb U\), the resulting list is linearly independent in \(\mathbb V\). Therefore, by Theorem-3.002: \[ \dim\mathbb U+1\le\dim\mathbb V. \] Thus: \[ \dim\mathbb U<\dim\mathbb V. \]

Definition: Sum of two subspaces \(\mathbb U_1+\mathbb U_2\). Let \(\mathbb U_1\) and \(\mathbb U_2\) be two subspaces of a vector space \(\mathbb V\). The sum of \(\mathbb U_1\) and \(\mathbb U_2\), denoted by \(\mathbb U_1 + \mathbb U_2\), is the set of all vectors that can be written as the sum of one vector from \(\mathbb U_1\) and one vector from \(\mathbb U_2\). That is,

\[ \mathbb U_1 + \mathbb U_2 = \left\{ \mathbf{u}_1+\mathbf{u}_2 \mid \mathbf{u}_1\in\mathbb U_1,\; \mathbf{u}_2\in\mathbb U_2 \right\}. \]

The sum \(\mathbb U_1+\mathbb U_2\) is itself a subspace of \(\mathbb V\).

We also use the notation \(\mathbb U_1\cap\mathbb U_2\), which is the set of all vectors that belong to both \(\mathbb U_1\) and \(\mathbb U_2\). That is: \[ \mathbb U_1\cap\mathbb U_2 = \{u\in\mathbb V\mid u\in\mathbb U_1 \text{ and }u\in\mathbb U_2\}. \] The intersection \(\mathbb U_1\cap\mathbb U_2\) is also a subspace of \(\mathbb V\). This is because it contains the zero vector and is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication, as both \(\mathbb U_1\) and \(\mathbb U_2\) have these properties.

Theorem-3.005: \(\mathbb{U}_1 + \mathbb{U}_2\) is a subspace.

Suppose \(\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(\mathbb{U}_2\) are two subspaces of a vector space \(\mathbb V\). Then their sum \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\), defined by \[ \mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2 = \{u_1+u_2\mid u_1\in\mathbb{U}_1,\;u_2\in\mathbb{U}_2\}, \] is also a subspace of \(\mathbb V\).

Proof:

In order to prove that \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\) is a subspace, we have to show that it contains the zero vector and is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication. First, since \(\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(\mathbb{U}_2\) are subspaces, we know that \(0\in\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(0\in\mathbb{U}_2\). Therefore: \[ 0=0+0\in\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2. \] Now let \(x,y\in\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\). Then by the definition of the sum, there exist \(u_1,v_1\in\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(u_2,v_2\in\mathbb{U}_2\) such that: \[ x=u_1+u_2,\qquad y=v_1+v_2. \] Therefore: \[ x+y=(u_1+u_2)+(v_1+v_2) =(u_1+v_1)+(u_2+v_2). \] Since \(\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(\mathbb{U}_2\) are subspaces, we have \(u_1+v_1\in\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(u_2+v_2\in\mathbb{U}_2\). Hence: \[ x+y\in\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2. \] So \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\) is closed under vector addition. Finally, let \(x\in\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\) and \(c\) be any scalar. Then there exist \(u_1\in\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(u_2\in\mathbb{U}_2\) such that \(x=u_1+u_2\). Therefore: \[ cx=c(u_1+u_2)=cu_1+cu_2. \] Since \(\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(\mathbb{U}_2\) are subspaces, we have \(cu_1\in\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(cu_2\in\mathbb{U}_2\). Thus: \[ cx\in\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2. \] Therefore \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\) contains the zero vector and is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication. Hence, by the definition of a subspace, \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\) is a subspace of \(\mathbb V\).

Now let us see what is the dimension of this new subspace \(\mathbb U_1+\mathbb U_2\). Below is a theorem that answers that. It might be a little bit unintuitive, but we will try our best to make sense of it.

Theorem-3.006: Dimension of a Sum

Suppose \(\mathbb V\) is a finite-dimensional vector space and \(\mathbb{U}_1,\mathbb{U}_2\) are subspaces of \(\mathbb V\). Then: \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2) = \dim(\mathbb{U}_1)+\dim(\mathbb{U}_2) -\dim(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2). \]

Proof:

By Theorem-3.004, \(\mathbb U_1\) and \(\mathbb U_2\) are finite-dimensional. Since their intersection is also a subspace of \(\mathbb V\), it is finite-dimensional as well.

Let \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) be a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2\). Then: \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2)=m. \] Since \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) is linearly independent in \(\mathbb{U}_1\), we can extend it to a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_1\). To see why this is possible, if the current list does not span \(\mathbb U_1\), we can add a vector from \(\mathbb U_1\) that is not in the span of the current list. This keeps the list linearly independent. Since \(\mathbb U_1\) is finite-dimensional, this process must eventually stop. Indeed, by Theorem-3.002, a linearly independent list cannot be longer than a finite spanning list of \(\mathbb U_1\). Once the list spans \(\mathbb U_1\), it is a basis.

Let this basis be: \[ u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j. \] Therefore: \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_1)=m+j. \] Similarly, we can extend \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) to a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_2\) using the same argument. Let this basis be: \[ u_1,\dots,u_m,w_1,\dots,w_k. \] Therefore: \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_2)=m+k. \]

We will now show that: \[ u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j,w_1,\dots,w_k \] is a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\). First, since \(u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(u_1,\dots,u_m,w_1,\dots,w_k\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_2\), every vector in \(\mathbb{U}_1\) and \(\mathbb{U}_2\) can be written as a linear combination of the vectors above. Therefore, every vector in \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\) can also be written as a linear combination of them. Thus: \[ \mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2 \subseteq \operatorname{span}(u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j,w_1,\dots,w_k). \] On the other hand, all these vectors belong to \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\), and this sum is a subspace by Theorem-3.005. Therefore: \[ \operatorname{span}(u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j,w_1,\dots,w_k) \subseteq \mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2. \] Hence: \[ \mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2 = \operatorname{span}(u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j,w_1,\dots,w_k). \] So it remains only to show that these vectors are linearly independent.

Suppose: \[ a_1u_1+\cdots+a_mu_m+b_1v_1+\cdots+b_jv_j +c_1w_1+\cdots+c_kw_k=0. \] We want to show that all the \(a\)'s, \(b\)'s, and \(c\)'s are equal to \(0\). We can rewrite the equation as: \[ c_1w_1+\cdots+c_kw_k = -a_1u_1-\cdots-a_mu_m-b_1v_1-\cdots-b_jv_j. \] The left side belongs to \(\mathbb{U}_2\), since \(w_1,\dots,w_k\in\mathbb{U}_2\). Therefore, the right side must also belong to \(\mathbb{U}_2\). But the right side is already a vector in \(\mathbb{U}_1\), since \(u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j\in\mathbb{U}_1\). Hence: \[ -a_1u_1-\cdots-a_mu_m-b_1v_1-\cdots-b_jv_j \in\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2. \] Since \(u_1,\dots,u_m\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2\), there exist scalars \(d_1,\dots,d_m\) such that: \[ -a_1u_1-\cdots-a_mu_m-b_1v_1-\cdots-b_jv_j = d_1u_1+\cdots+d_mu_m. \] Rearranging gives: \[ (a_1+d_1)u_1+\cdots+(a_m+d_m)u_m +b_1v_1+\cdots+b_jv_j=0. \] But \(u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j\) is a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_1\), and therefore it is linearly independent. Thus: \[ a_i+d_i=0\qquad (i=1,\dots,m) \] and: \[ b_1=\cdots=b_j=0. \]

Now recall that we established: \[ c_1w_1+\cdots+c_kw_k = d_1u_1+\cdots+d_mu_m. \] Rearranging gives: \[ c_1w_1+\cdots+c_kw_k -d_1u_1-\cdots-d_mu_m=0. \] Since \(u_1,\dots,u_m,w_1,\dots,w_k\) form a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_2\), they are linearly independent. Hence: \[ c_1=\cdots=c_k=0 \] and: \[ d_1=\cdots=d_m=0. \] But we already established that: \[ a_i+d_i=0\qquad (i=1,\dots,m). \] Therefore: \[ a_1=\cdots=a_m=0. \] Together with \(b_1=\cdots=b_j=0\) and \(c_1=\cdots=c_k=0\), all the coefficients are \(0\). Hence: \[ u_1,\dots,u_m,v_1,\dots,v_j,w_1,\dots,w_k \] is linearly independent.

Thus it is a basis of \(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2\), and so: \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2)=m+j+k. \] Finally, since: \[ \begin{aligned} \dim(\mathbb{U}_1)&=m+j,\\ \dim(\mathbb{U}_2)&=m+k,\\ \dim(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2)&=m, \end{aligned} \] we get: \[ \begin{aligned} \dim(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2) &=m+j+k\\ &=(m+j)+(m+k)-m\\ &=\dim(\mathbb{U}_1)+\dim(\mathbb{U}_2) -\dim(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2). \end{aligned} \] This proves the theorem.

To make sense of this theorem, consider \(\mathbb R^3\), and suppose two planes are passing through the origin. They need to pass through the origin so that they are each a subspace of \(\mathbb R^3\), since they contain the origin. Then, since they both pass through the origin, two things can happen: either both planes coincide, that is \(\mathbb U_1=\mathbb U_2\), or they are distinct, in which case their intersection is a line.

To see why the intersection of two distinct planes is a line, notice that each plane has dimension \(2\). Their sum is a subspace of \(\mathbb R^3\), so by Theorem-3.004 its dimension cannot exceed \(3\). By Theorem-3.006: \[ \dim(\mathbb U_1\cap\mathbb U_2) = 2+2-\dim(\mathbb U_1+\mathbb U_2) \geq 1. \] Since the planes are distinct, their intersection cannot itself be a plane. Therefore, the intersection has dimension exactly \(1\), meaning that it is a line.

For example, imagine the two planes to be the \(xy\)-plane and the \(yz\)-plane. Their intersection is the \(y\)-axis. In this case, their sum is the whole \(\mathbb R^3\), so: \[ \mathbb U_1+\mathbb U_2=\mathbb R^3. \] We can see this explicitly by considering the following three vectors: \[ \mathbf e_1= \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf e_2= \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf e_3= \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}. \] Here, \(\mathbf e_1\) belongs to the \(xy\)-plane, \(\mathbf e_2\) belongs to both planes, and \(\mathbf e_3\) belongs to the \(yz\)-plane. These three vectors are linearly independent and span \(\mathbb R^3\).

In the other case, when both planes coincide, we get: \[ \mathbb U_1+\mathbb U_2=\mathbb U_1=\mathbb U_2. \] Also: \[ \mathbb U_1\cap\mathbb U_2 = \mathbb U_1 = \mathbb U_2. \] Therefore: \[ \dim(\mathbb U_1+\mathbb U_2) = \dim\mathbb U_1 = \dim\mathbb U_2. \] By the theorem: \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2) = \dim(\mathbb{U}_1)+\dim(\mathbb{U}_2) -\dim(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2). \] Here: \[ \dim(\mathbb U_1\cap\mathbb U_2) = \dim\mathbb U_1 = \dim\mathbb U_2. \] Therefore, we get: \[ \begin{aligned} \dim(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2) &= \dim(\mathbb{U}_1)+\dim(\mathbb{U}_2) -\dim(\mathbb{U}_1\cap\mathbb{U}_2)\\ &= \dim(\mathbb{U}_1)+\dim(\mathbb{U}_2) -\dim(\mathbb U_1)\\ &=\dim(\mathbb U_2), \end{aligned} \] as expected. Similarly, since \(\dim(\mathbb U_1\cap\mathbb U_2)=\dim(\mathbb U_2)\), we can also write: \[ \begin{aligned} \dim(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2) &= \dim(\mathbb{U}_1)+\dim(\mathbb{U}_2) -\dim(\mathbb U_2)\\ &=\dim(\mathbb U_1), \end{aligned} \] which is again exactly what we expected.

In the other case, when they only intersect in a line, we get: \[ \dim(\mathbb U_1\cap\mathbb U_2)=1. \] Since both are planes in \(\mathbb R^3\), we also know that: \[ \dim(\mathbb U_1)=\dim(\mathbb U_2)=2. \] So by the theorem: \[ \dim(\mathbb{U}_1+\mathbb{U}_2)=2+2-1=3. \] Therefore, their sum has dimension \(3\). Since it is a subspace of \(\mathbb R^3\), it must be the whole space: \[ \mathbb U_1+\mathbb U_2=\mathbb R^3. \]
