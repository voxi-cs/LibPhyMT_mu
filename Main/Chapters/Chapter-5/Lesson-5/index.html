Introduction

In this lesson we will construct a rigorous definition of the integral, where we use the ideas of measure theory to deal with the drawbacks of the Riemann integral. We will discuss the Lebesgue integral and how it deals with the drawbacks of the Riemann integral, and we will formalize the definition of the integral in the general case of \(\mathbb R\). Later, in the product measure lesson, we will see how our idea of integration generalizes to higher dimensions.

Riemann Integration

Let us first look at Riemann integration and its drawbacks so that we can see how measure theory allows us to deal with those drawbacks. Even though you are most likely familiar with the formal definition of Riemann integration, we would still like to put it here just in case.

Definition: Partition \(P\) Assume \(a < b\), where \(a,b \in \mathbb R\). A partition of the closed interval \([a,b]\) is a finite list of the form \(P = \{x_0,x_1,\dots,x_n\}\), such that: \[ a = x_0 < x_1 < \dots < x_n = b. \] Thus, \[ [a,b] = [x_0,x_1] \cup [x_1,x_2] \cup \dots \cup [x_{n-1},x_n]. \]

Then the upper and lower Riemann sums are defined as:

Definition: Upper and Lower Riemann Sums Assume \(f : [a,b] \to \mathbb R\) is a bounded function, and let \(P\) be a partition of \([a,b]\). Then the upper and lower Riemann sums are defined as: \[ U(f,P) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (x_j-x_{j-1}) \sup_{[x_{j-1},x_j]} f \] and \[ L(f,P) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (x_j-x_{j-1}) \inf_{[x_{j-1},x_j]} f. \]

We know that \(U(f,P) \ge L(f,P)\). After this, we define the upper and lower Riemann integrals as:

Definition: Upper and Lower Riemann Integrals Assume \(f : [a,b] \to \mathbb R\) is a bounded function. Then the upper and lower Riemann integrals are defined as: \[ \overline{\int_a^b} f(x)\,dx = \inf_{P} U(f,P) \] and \[ \underline{\int_a^b} f(x)\,dx = \sup_{P} L(f,P). \] Here the infimum and supremum are taken over all partitions \(P\) of \([a,b]\).

Finally, a function \(f\) is said to be Riemann integrable if and only if its upper and lower Riemann integrals are equal. Thus, we define Riemann integration as:

Definition: Riemann Integrable and Riemann Integral A bounded function \(f: [a,b] \to \mathbb R\) is said to be Riemann integrable if and only if \[ \inf_P U(f,P) = \sup_P L(f,P), \] and the Riemann integral is defined as: \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \inf_P U(f,P) = \sup_P L(f,P). \]

Issues with the Riemann Integral

There are three main theoretical limitations of the Riemann integral, as follows:

  1. Although the Riemann integral can be generalized to higher dimensions, its formulation becomes increasingly cumbersome for complicated domains and functions. Measure theory provides a more general and systematic framework for integration that works naturally in arbitrary dimensions and beyond.
  2. Riemann integration is poorly suited to functions with many discontinuities.
  3. The pointwise limit of a sequence of Riemann integrable functions need not be Riemann integrable.

As we said, the Riemann integral, when working in general with arbitrary subsets in higher dimensions, can become technical. In \(\mathbb R\), we mainly work with intervals when integrating bounded continuous functions. But in \(\mathbb R^2\), for example, things become more complicated when the region of integration is arbitrary. For example, consider finding the area of a circle with radius \(r\), where \[ D = \{(x,y): x^2 + y^2 \le r^2\}. \] We can certainly handle this using Riemann integration. However, consider a more complicated region, such as \[ D = \left\{(x,y):0 \lt x\le1,\ 0\le y\le 1+\sin(1/x)\right\}, \] and suppose we are given a function \(f:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R\) and asked to find the volume under the surface over \(D\). We can indeed calculate this using Riemann integration, but the complicated and infinitely oscillating boundary makes the calculation more cumbersome. In such cases, Lebesgue integration provides a more convenient and general framework, allowing much of this technicality to be avoided.

As we move to higher dimensions, the subsets of such spaces can become even more complicated, as we will see in the lesson on product measure. In such cases, Riemann integration is not always the most convenient choice, and we can instead use the more general framework of Lebesgue integration. Moreover, the framework of measure theory extends naturally beyond \(\mathbb R^n\) altogether — to abstract spaces such as spaces of functions or probability spaces — where the notion of a Riemann integral does not even make sense.


Now let us show an example of a function that is discontinuous on \(\mathbb{R}\) everywhere: \[ f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x \notin \mathbb{Q} \\ 0, & x \in \mathbb{Q} \end{cases} \]

It is easy to see that this is not Riemann integrable. Remember \(\mathbb Q\) the set of rational numbers and \(\mathbb I\) the set of irrational numbers are both dense in \(\mathbb R\), that means for all intervals there are always some rational numbers and irrational numbers. Then that means: \[ \inf_{[x_{j-1}, x_j]} f(x) = 0 \] As the lowest possible value of \(f\) is zero and we have at least one rational number in the interval \([x_{j-1}, x_j]\) for arbitrary values of \(x_{j-1}, x_j \in \mathbb R\). Similarly: \[ \sup_{[x_{j-1}, x_j]} f(x) = 1 \] As the set of irrational numbers is also dense in \(\mathbb R\), and \(f = 1\) on all irrationals.

Let us just consider the Riemann integral of this function on \([0,1]\), then the upper Riemann sum is: \[ U(f,P,[0,1]) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \left(\sup_{I_j} f\right) (x_j -x_{j-1}) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} (x_j -x_{j-1}) = (x_1 - x_0) + (x_2 - x_1) + \dots + (x_{n} - x_{n-1}) = x_n - x_0 = 1 - 0 = 1. \] Thus the upper Riemann sum is \(\inf_{P} U(f,P,[0,1]) = 1\). And the lower Riemann sum is: \[ L(f,P,[0,1]) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \left(\inf_{I_j} f\right) (x_j -x_{j-1}) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} 0(x_j -x_{j-1}) = 0. \] For \(f(x)\) to be Riemann integrable we need \[ \inf_{P} U(f,P,[0,1]) = \sup_{P} L(f,P,[0,1]) \] But here we have \(\inf_{P} U(f,P,[0,1]) = 1\) and \(\sup_{P} L(f,P,[0,1]) = 0\), since \(\inf_{P} U(f,P,[0,1]) \neq \sup_{P} L(f,P,[0,1])\) we can see that the function \[ f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x \notin \mathbb{Q} \\ 0, & x \in \mathbb{Q} \end{cases} \] is not Riemann integrable, this function is also known as the Dirichlet function.

Dirichlet function as we can see is not remine intigrable, but soon we will see lebusge intigral of Dirichlet function is \(1\). \[ \int f \, \text{d}\mu = 1 \] Thus as we said "Riemann integration is poorly suited to functions with many discontinuities.".


Now let us look at another important issue with Riemann integration, consider the function: \[ f_k(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x = \frac{p}{q} \in [0,1], \, \gcd(p,q) = 1, \, q\le k, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

Let us see for \(k \in \mathbb Z^{+}\) the Riemann integration of \(f_k\), since \(f_k\) only gives points that are discontinuous we get: \[ \int_{0}^{1} f_k(x) \, \text{d}x = 0 \] for all \(k \in \mathbb Z^{+}\), the question we want to ask is what happens if we let \(k\) approach infinity.

Notice as \(k \to \infty\) we get the Dirichlet function. So can we say: \[ \lim_{k\to \infty} \int_{0}^{1} f_k(x) \, \text{d}x = 0 = \int_{0}^{1} \lim_{k\to \infty} f_k(x) \, \text{d}x \] Unfortunately no, as you can already see \(\lim_{k\to \infty} f_k(x) = f(x)\) which is the Dirichlet function, and we have just seen the Dirichlet function is not Riemann integrable, thus Riemann integration doesn't necessarily imply that if \(f_k \to f\) then: \[ \lim_{k\to \infty} \int f_k(x) \, \text{d}x = \int \lim_{k\to \infty} f_k(x) \, \text{d}x \] For Riemann integration the above equality holds certainly for a sequence of \(f_k, f\) if they are bounded on the same bounded interval and converge uniformly, but for pointwise convergence the equality above doesn't hold in general.

Integration with Respect to a Measure

Before we define the intigration we frist need to define partiation as what we did in the case of remine intgration, but ofcouse here the partion are going to be diffrent and each partiation \(A_i\) is not nessecarly a interval or any specifc kind of set, we only need it to be \(\mathcal S\)-measuraable.

Definition: \(\mathcal S\)-Partition Suppose we have a \(\sigma\)-algbera \(\mathcal S\) on set \(X\), then \(\mathcal S\)-partiation is a finite collection of sets \(A_1,\dots, A_n\) that are in \(\mathcal S\),in other words \(\mathcal S\)-measurable, such that: \[ A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_n = X. \]

Note, this definition is for an arbitrary set \(X\), and it does not have to be \(\mathbb R\). In the case when \(X = \mathbb R\) and we have the Lebesgue measure \(\mu\), we do not necessarily need partitions that are closed intervals; the only thing they need to be is measurable so that we can measure them using the Lebesgue measure \(\mu\).

Let us now define the lower sum, which was introduced by Henri Lebesgue and that is why it is called the Lower Lebesgue Sum. This does not mean we are working specifically with Lebesgue measure; the following is defined on an arbitrary measure space \((X,\mathcal S,\mu)\). It is important to mention here that all the functions we are dealing with are nonnegative functions. Later, we will generalize everything to general functions by writing a function as the difference of its positive and negative parts, \(f = f^+ - f^-\), whenever the difference of their integrals is well-defined. So keep in mind everything here is defined for nonnegative functions.

Definition: Lower Lebesgue Sum Suppose \((X,\mathcal S, \mu)\) is a measure space, and the function \(f: X \to [0,\infty]\) is \(\mathcal S\)-measurable and \(P = \{A_1, A_2,\dots, A_n\}\) is a finite \(\mathcal S\)-partition of \(X\). Then the lower Lebesgue sum is defined as: \[ \mathcal L (f , P ) = \sum_{j=1}^{n} \mu(A_j) \inf_{A_j} f. \]

Now we will define the integral of a nonnegative function. Like in the case of Riemann integration, here we will write integration using \(\int\), except we won't use \(\text{d}x\); instead, we will use \(\text{d}\mu\) to specify that the integration is done with respect to the measure \(\mu\) on the measure space \((X,\mathcal S,\mu)\). However, the definition should be such that the following two important conditions are satisfied: \[ \int \chi_{E} \, \text{d}\mu = \mu(E), \quad \forall E\in\mathcal S, \] and \[ \int (f+g)\,\text{d}\mu = \int f\,\text{d}\mu+\int g\,\text{d}\mu. \] The second condition is important because it gives us the additivity of the integral, while the first condition connects integration with measure itself: the integral of the indicator function of a measurable set \(E\) should give the measure of that set. In other words, integrating \(\chi_E\) should simply give us the "size" of \(E\), just as measuring \(E\) with \(\mu\) does. The importance of the first condition may not be immediately apparent at first glance, but it will become much clearer when we define the integral of simple functions. In fact, this condition is what allows us to extend the idea of measuring sets to integrating functions while preserving the essential properties we expect an integral to have.

Definition: Integral of a Nonnegative Function Suppose \((X,\mathcal S, \mu)\) is a measure space and \(f: X \to [0,\infty]\) is an \(\mathcal S\)-measurable function. Then the integral of \(f\) is defined by: \[ \int f \, \text{d}\mu = \sup \{ \mathcal{L}(f, P): P \text{ is a finite } \mathcal S\text{-partition of } X\}. \]

Now we will show you that our definition indeed assures that the first condition \(\int \chi_{E} \, \text{d}\mu = \mu(E), \text{ } \forall E\in\mathcal S\) is satisfied.

Theorem-5.032

Suppose \((X, \mathcal S, \mu)\) is a measure space and \(E \in \mathcal S\), then: \[ \int \chi_{E} \, \text{d}\mu = \mu(E). \]

Proof:

Let \(P = \{E , X \setminus E \}\) be the \(\mathcal S\)-partition of \(X\). Since \(\chi_E = 1\) on \(E\) and \(\chi_E = 0\) on \(X \setminus E\), we can say: \[ \mathcal L(\chi_E , P ) = \mu(E)\cdot 1 + \mu(X \setminus E) \cdot 0 = \mu(E). \] Since \(\int \chi_E \, \text{d}\mu \) is the supremum of \(\mathcal L(\chi_E , P )\) over all finite \(\mathcal S\)-partitions \(P\), then: \[ \int \chi_E \, \text{d}\mu \ge \mu(E). \]

Now we need to prove the reverse, that \(\int \chi_E \, \text{d}\mu \le \mu(E)\). For that, let \(P = \{A_1, \dots , A_n\}\) be an arbitrary finite \(\mathcal S\)-partition of \(X\). For each \(A_j\), since \(\chi_E\) is zero at every point outside \(E\), we can say: \[ \mu(A_j) \inf_{A_j} \chi_E = \begin{cases} \mu(A_j) & \text{if } A_j \subset E, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \] Therefore: \[ \mathcal L(\chi_{E}, P) = \sum_{j: A_j \subset E} \mu(A_j) = \mu\left( \bigcup_{j: A_j \subset E} A_j \right) \le \mu(E). \] Since \(P\) was chosen arbitrarily, that implies: \[ \int \chi_E \, \text{d}\mu \le \mu(E). \] Therefore we can say: \[ \int \chi_E \, \text{d}\mu = \mu(E). \]

Now we have proven that our definition of integration with respect to a measure assures that Theorem-5.0## is satisfied. Now let us go back to the Dirichlet function. We said that it is integrable with respect to Lebesgue measure. Now we see in the example below the integration of the Dirichlet function with respect to Lebesgue measure.

EXAMPLE

Given the Dirichlet function below on the interval \([0,1]\), find its integration with respect to Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\). (Note: we represent Lebesgue measure as \(\lambda\)). \[ f(x) = \chi_{[0,1]\setminus \mathbb Q}(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & x \in [0,1]\setminus \mathbb Q \\ 0, & x \in \mathbb{Q} \end{cases} \]

Proof:

We know: \[ \int \chi_E \, \text{d}\mu = \mu(E). \] Here we have: \[ \int_{[0,1]} f(x) \, \text{d}\lambda = \int_{[0,1]} \chi_{[0,1]\setminus \mathbb Q}(x) \, \text{d}\lambda = \lambda([0,1]\setminus \mathbb Q). \] We can write the interval \([0,1]\) as the union of two disjoint sets: \[ [0,1] = ([0,1] \cap \mathbb Q) \cup ([0,1] \setminus \mathbb Q) \] We know that if we have disjoint sets \(E_1,\dots,E_n\), then: \[ \mu\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{n} E_k\right) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \mu(E_k) \] Here we have \(E_1 = ([0,1] \cap \mathbb Q)\) and \(E_2 = ([0,1] \setminus \mathbb Q)\), and Lebesgue measure \(\lambda\). Therefore, since \([0,1] = E_1 \cup E_2\): \[ \lambda([0,1]) = \lambda(E_1) + \lambda(E_2) \] Since \([0,1]\) is Borel and Lebesgue measure is the outer measure restricted to Borel subsets, we have \(\lambda([0,1]) = 1\). \[ \Rightarrow 1 = \lambda([0,1] \cap \mathbb Q) + \lambda([0,1] \setminus \mathbb Q) \] Here the set \([0,1] \cap \mathbb Q\) is the set of rational numbers on the interval \([0,1]\), and we know the Lebesgue measure of \(\mathbb Q\) is zero. Therefore, we get \(\lambda([0,1] \cap \mathbb Q) = 0\), so we have: \[ \Rightarrow 0 + \lambda([0,1] \setminus \mathbb Q) = 1 \implies \lambda([0,1] \setminus \mathbb Q) = 1 \] And that is exactly what we need to solve the integral, so we get that the Dirichlet function is integrable with respect to Lebesgue measure, and: \[ \int_{[0,1]} f(x) \, \text{d}\lambda = \int_{[0,1]} \chi_{[0,1]\setminus \mathbb Q}(x) \, \text{d}\lambda = \lambda([0,1]\setminus \mathbb Q) = 1 \]

The integration with respect to a measure is called Lebesgue integration, and it doesn't necessarily mean we are working with the Lebesgue measure.

Let us explore some properties (as theorems) of Lebesgue integration as a result of choosing the first condition \(\int \chi_E \, \text{d}\mu = \mu(E)\) to hold. Then soon it will be clear why we choose that particular condition and, based on that and the other conditions, how we defined the Lebesgue integration.

Now let us talk about simple functions. Simple functions are functions of the form \(f(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}\). When we let the \(E_k\)'s be disjoint intervals in \(X\) and plot \(f\), we get a bunch of vertical rectangles, with height \(c_k\), and thus we hope to get its integration to be the sum of the heights \(c_k\) times the measure of the intervals \(\mu(E_k)\). But of course, the \(E_k\)'s are not necessarily intervals and can be arbitrary measurable subsets of \(X\), but the idea of integration still holds. Thus, the theorem below assures our expectation.

Theorem-5.033

Suppose \((X, \mathcal S, \mu)\) is a measure space, let \(E_1,\dots,E_n\) be disjoint sets in \(\mathcal S\), and \(c_1,\dots,c_n \in [0,\infty)\). Then: \[ \int \left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}\right) \, \text{d}\mu = \sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \mu(E_k) \]

Proof:

Assume we have the \(\mathcal S\)-partition of \(X\): \[ P = \{E_1, \dots, E_n, E_{n+1}\}, \text{ where } E_{n+1} = X \setminus (E_1 \cup \dots \cup E_n) \] Here we can choose \(P\) as a partition because of \(E_{n+1}\), and also because \(E_1,\dots,E_n\) are all measurable, and \(E_{n+1}\) is measurable because we know if \(E \in \mathcal S \implies X \setminus E \in \mathcal S\). Alright, now that we have this particular \(\mathcal S\)-partition, we can say: \[ \mathcal{L} \left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}, P\right) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \mu(E_k) \] Note: Here we ignored \(E_{n+1}\) by choosing \(c_{n+1} = 0\), since we only choose \(E_{n+1}\) for the partition.

Since integration is the supremum of \(\mathcal L\), then we can say: \[ \int \left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}\right) \, \text{d}\mu \ge \sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \mu(E_k) \] Now that we have \(\ge\), we need to prove the other way \(\le\).

Choose an arbitrary \(\mathcal S\)-partition \(P = \{A_1,\dots,A_m\}\) of \(X\). Then: \[ \mathcal L\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}, P\right) = \sum_{j=1}^{m} \mu(A_j) \min_{\{i : A_j \cap E_i \neq \varnothing\}} c_i, \] where we include \(E_{n+1}\) with \(c_{n+1}=0\). Thus, more precisely: \[ \mathcal L\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}, P\right) = \sum_{j=1}^{m} \mu(A_j) \min_{\{i : A_j \cap E_i \neq \varnothing\}} c_i, \] where \(i\) ranges over \(1,\dots,n+1\). Here we had to take \(\min_{\{i : A_j \cap E_i \neq \varnothing\}} c_i\) because we need the infimum of \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}\) over \(A_j\)'s. The function \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}\) takes the value \(c_i\) on \(E_i\) for \(i=1,\dots,n\), and it takes the value \(0=c_{n+1}\) on \(E_{n+1}\). Therefore, by taking the smallest \(c_i\) over all \(E_i\) such that \(A_j \cap E_i \neq \varnothing\), we get the infimum of \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}\) over \(A_j\). Alright, now we can write: \[ \mu(A_j) = \sum_{k=1}^{n+1} \mu(A_j \cap E_k) \] since \(E_1,\dots,E_{n+1}\) form a partition of \(X\). So we get: \[ \implies \mathcal L\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}, P\right) = \sum_{j=1}^{m} \sum_{k=1}^{n+1} \mu(A_j \cap E_k) \min_{\{i : A_j \cap E_i \neq \varnothing\}} c_i \] \[ \implies \mathcal L\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}, P\right) \le \sum_{j=1}^{m} \sum_{k=1}^{n+1} \mu(A_j \cap E_k)c_k \] Since \(c_{n+1}=0\), the \(k=n+1\) term is \(0\), so: \[ \implies \mathcal L\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}, P\right) \le \sum_{j=1}^{m} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \mu(A_j \cap E_k)c_k = \sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \sum_{j=1}^{m}\mu(A_j \cap E_k) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \mu(E_k) \] since \(A_1,\dots,A_m\) form a partition of \(X\). Therefore: \[ \mathcal L\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}, P\right) \le \sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \mu(E_k) \] Since the integration is the supremum over all partitions \(P\), we get: \[ \mathcal L\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}, P\right) \le \sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \mu(E_k) \implies \int \left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}\right) \, \text{d}\mu \le \sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \mu(E_k) \] And therefore we have: \[ \int \left(\sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \chi_{E_k}\right) \, \text{d}\mu = \sum_{k=1}^{n} c_k \mu(E_k) \]

Now it is time to discuss a particular theorem called the Monotone Convergence Theorem (M.C.T.). This theorem is a first step toward addressing the second issue we encountered with the Riemann integral: the pointwise limit of a sequence of Riemann integrable functions need not be Riemann integrable. However, the M.C.T. applies only to sequences of non-negative functions that increase pointwise, meaning \[ 0 \leq f_1(x) \leq f_2(x) \leq \cdots. \] Therefore, it does not apply to every general sequence of functions. Nevertheless, it gives an important result for passing the limit through the Lebesgue integral.

Monotone Convergence Theorem

Before we prove the M.C.T., we need to show the equivalence between the two definitions of the Lebesgue integral. The definition is as follows:

Definition Suppose \((X,\mathcal S, \mu)\) is a measure space, and \(f: X \to [0,\infty]\) is an \(\mathcal S\)-measurable function. Then: \[ \int f \, \text{d}\mu = \sup \left\{ \sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j \mu(A_j) \;:\; \text{disjoint sets } A_1,\dots,A_n \in \mathcal S \text{ and } c_1,\dots,c_n \in [0,\infty) \text{ such that } f(x) \ge \sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j \chi_{A_j}, \ \forall x \in X \right\} \]

The definition is saying essentially the same thing as our previous definition of the Lebesgue integral, but this form will be useful when we approach the proof of the M.C.T. What this definition is saying is that we consider all possible combinations of disjoint measurable sets \(A_1,\dots,A_n\) and non-negative constants \(c_1,\dots,c_n\) such that \(f(x) \geq \sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j\chi_{A_j}\), \(\forall x \in X\). Each such combination gives us a simple function \(\sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j\chi_{A_j}\) that lies below \(f\). For each of these simple functions, we calculate \(\sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j\mu(A_j)\). This gives us a set of possible values, and the Lebesgue integral is defined as the supremum of all these values. Notice that we do not need any particular simple function to actually attain the supremum. The supremum is simply the least upper bound of all the values obtained from these possible simple functions.

Nevertheless, we still need to prove the equivalence between this definition of the Lebesgue integral and the previous definition. Thus, we have the following:

Theorem-5.034

\[ \sup\left\{ \sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j \mu(A_j) \, : \, \text{ disjoint sets } A_1,\dots,A_n \in \mathcal S,\ c_1,\dots,c_n \in [0,\infty),\ f(x) \ge \sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j \chi(A_j),\ \forall x \in X\right\} = \sup \{ \mathcal{L}(f, P): P \text{ is an } \mathcal S \text{-partition of } X\}. \]

Proof:

First, let \(P\) be an \(\mathcal S\)-partition of \(X\). Suppose first that \(\inf_A f \lt \infty\) for every \(A\in\mathcal S\) with \(\mu(A) \gt 0\). For every nonempty set \(A_j\) in \(P\) with \(\mu(A_j) \gt 0\), take \[ c_j=\inf_{A_j}f, \] and for the sets with \(\mu(A_j)=0\), take \(c_j=0\). Then \[ \sum_{j=1}^{n}c_j\chi(A_j)\leq f \] on \(X\), since \(f\geq0\). Therefore, \[ \sum_{j=1}^{n}c_j\mu(A_j) \] is one of the sums allowed on the left side, and this sum is exactly \(\mathcal L(f,P)\). Hence, for every \(\mathcal S\)-partition \(P\), \[ \mathcal L(f,P) \leq \sup\left\{ \sum_{j=1}^{n} c_j \mu(A_j) : \text{disjoint sets } A_1,\dots,A_n\in\mathcal S,\ c_1,\dots,c_n\in[0,\infty) \text{ satisfying the given condition} \right\}. \] Taking the supremum over all \(\mathcal S\)-partitions \(P\), we obtain that the right side of the theorem is less than or equal to the left side.

Now suppose there exists \(A\in\mathcal S\) with \[ \mu(A) \gt 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \inf_A f=\infty. \] Then \(f(x)=\infty\) for every \(x\in A\). For any \(t\in[0,\infty)\), take \(n=1\), \(A_1=A\), and \(c_1=t\). Since \[ f(x)\geq t\chi_A(x) \] for every \(x\in X\), the left side of the theorem is at least \(t\mu(A)\). Because \(\mu(A)\gt0\) and \(t\) can be arbitrarily large, the left side equals \(\infty\). On the other hand, taking the \(\mathcal S\)-partition \[ P=\{A,X\setminus A\}, \] we have \[ \mathcal L(f,P)\geq(\inf_A f)\mu(A)=\infty. \] Thus the right side is also \(\infty\). Therefore, the desired equality holds in this case as well.

Now we show the reverse inequality. Suppose that \[ \inf_A f \lt \infty \] for every \(A\in\mathcal S\) with \(\mu(A)\gt0\). Take any disjoint sets \(A_1,\dots,A_n\in\mathcal S\) and numbers \(c_1,\dots,c_n\in[0,\infty)\) such that \[ f(x)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{n}c_j\chi(A_j) \] for every \(x\in X\). For every \(A_j\) with \(\mu(A_j)\gt0\), evaluating the condition on \(A_j\) gives \[ c_j\leq\inf_{A_j}f. \] Therefore, \[ \sum_{\mu(A_j)>0}c_j\mu(A_j) \leq \sum_{\mu(A_j)>0}(\inf_{A_j}f)\mu(A_j). \] Sets with \(\mu(A_j)=0\) do not contribute to either side, so \[ \sum_{j=1}^{n}c_j\mu(A_j) \leq \sum_{\mu(A_j)>0}(\inf_{A_j}f)\mu(A_j). \] We extend \(A_1,\dots,A_n\) to an \(\mathcal S\)-partition \(P\) of \(X\) by adjoining the set \[ X\setminus(A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_n) \] as an additional element, whenever it is nonempty. By the definition of \(\mathcal L(f,P)\), \[ \sum_{\mu(A_j)>0}(\inf_{A_j}f)\mu(A_j) \leq \mathcal L(f,P). \] Hence, \[ \sum_{j=1}^{n}c_j\mu(A_j) \leq \mathcal L(f,P) \leq \sup\{\mathcal L(f,P):P\text{ is an }\mathcal S\text{-partition of }X\}. \] Since the sets \(A_1,\dots,A_n\) and scalars \(c_1,\dots,c_n\) were arbitrary, taking the supremum over all such choices shows that the left side of the theorem is less than or equal to the right side. Thus equality holds.

Now that we have proven that indeed both definitions shown above are equivalent, we are now ready to use this new definition to prove the M.C.T.

Theorem-5.035: Monotone Convergence Theorem(M.C.T)

Suppose \((X,\mathcal S, \mu)\) is a measure sapce, and a sequence of non nagative increasing functions \(0\le f_1 \le f_2 \le \dots\) that are \(\mathcal S\)-measurable. Then: \[ \lim_{k \to \infty} \int f_k \, \text{d}\mu = \int f \, \text{d} \mu \] Where, \(f: X \to [0,\infty]\) a function defined by: \[ f(x) = \lim_{k \to \infty} f_k(x) \]

Proof:

Since each \(f_k\) satisfies \(f_k(x) \le f(x)\) for every \(x \in X\) — the sequence is non-decreasing and \(f\) is its pointwise limit — the monotonicity of the integral gives us \(\int f_k \, \text{d}\mu \le \int f \, \text{d}\mu\) for every \(k \in \mathbb{Z}^+\). Since this inequality holds for every \(k\), it carries through to the limit, and so we can say that: \[ \lim_{k \to \infty} \int f_k \, \text{d}\mu \le \int f \, \text{d}\mu \] Now we need to establish the inequality in the opposite direction, and this is the part that requires more work. To do so, let \(A_1, \dots, A_m\) be any disjoint collection of sets in \(\mathcal{S}\), and let \(c_1, \dots, c_m \in [0, \infty)\) be any non-negative constants such that: \[ f(x) \ge \sum_{j=1}^{m} c_j \chi_{A_j}(x) \quad \text{for every } x \in X \] Choose any \(t \in (0,1)\), and for each \(k \in \mathbb{Z}^+\), define: \[ E_k = \left\{ x \in X : f_k(x) \ge t\sum_{j=1}^{m} c_j \chi_{A_j}(x) \right\} \] The reason why we define this particular set \(E_k\) is that it collects exactly those points in \(X\) where \(f_k\) has already grown large enough to be at least the fraction \(t\) of the simple function on the right. Because the sequence \(f_1 \le f_2 \le \dots\) is non-decreasing, any point that belongs to \(E_k\) will remain in \(E_{k+1}, E_{k+2},\) and all subsequent sets, giving us the increasing chain: \[ E_1 \subset E_2 \subset \cdots \] We also claim that \(\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} E_k = X\). To see why, take any point \(x \in X\). Since \(f_k(x) \to f(x)\) as \(k \to \infty\) and \(f(x) \ge \sum_{j=1}^{m} c_j \chi_{A_j}(x)\), and since \(t \lt 1\), we can always find a large enough \(k\) so that \(f_k(x)\) has grown beyond \(t \cdot \sum_{j=1}^{m} c_j \chi_{A_j}(x)\), meaning \(x \in E_k\). Since \(x\) was an arbitrary point of \(X\), we conclude that \(\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} E_k = X\). Hence, \(E_1 \subset E_2 \subset \cdots\) is an increasing sequence of measurable sets in \(\mathcal{S}\) whose union is all of \(X\), and therefore for each \(j \in \{1, \dots, m\}\): \[ \lim_{k \to \infty} \mu(A_j \cap E_k) = \mu(A_j) \] Now, for any fixed \(k \in \mathbb{Z}^+\), the definition of \(E_k\) tells us that for every \(x \in X\): \[ f_k(x) \ge \sum_{j=1}^{m} tc_j \chi_{A_j \cap E_k}(x) \] The reason why we can write it this way is that for any \(x \in A_j \cap E_k\), membership in \(E_k\) guarantees \(f_k(x) \ge tc_j\), while for any \(x\) outside of \(E_k\), the right-hand side simply evaluates to zero. Integrating both sides, we get: \[ \int f_k \, \text{d}\mu \ge t\sum_{j=1}^{m} c_j\,\mu(A_j \cap E_k) \] If we now take the limit as \(k \to \infty\) of both sides, and use the fact that \(\mu(A_j \cap E_k) \to \mu(A_j)\) for each \(j\), we get: \[ \lim_{k \to \infty} \int f_k \, \text{d}\mu \ge t\sum_{j=1}^{m} c_j\,\mu(A_j) \] Since \(t\) was any number in \((0,1)\), we can now let \(t\) increase towards \(1\). Because the left-hand side does not depend on \(t\) at all, as \(t \to 1\) the right-hand side approaches \(\sum_{j=1}^{m} c_j\,\mu(A_j)\), and therefore we conclude that: \[ \lim_{k \to \infty} \int f_k \, \text{d}\mu \ge \sum_{j=1}^{m} c_j\,\mu(A_j) \] Since this holds for every valid choice of disjoint sets \(A_1, \dots, A_m \in \mathcal{S}\) and non-negative constants \(c_1, \dots, c_m\) satisfying \(f(x) \ge \sum_{j=1}^m c_j \chi_{A_j}(x)\), if we now take the supremum over all such choices, we obtain exactly the integral of \(f\) by definition, and so it follows that: \[ \lim_{k \to \infty} \int f_k \, \text{d}\mu \ge \int f \, \text{d}\mu \] Combining this with the very first inequality we showed, we can therefore conclude that: \[ \lim_{k \to \infty} \int f_k \, \text{d}\mu = \int f \, \text{d}\mu \]

Well now we have proven the M.C.T., it is certainly true that we can interchange the limit and the integral for a specific class of sequences of functions, that is, when \(0\le f_1 \le f_2 \le \dots\). But there are stronger theorems, as we will see in the next lesson, such as the Bounded Convergence Theorem and Dominated Convergence Theorem, which apply to other classes of functions and allow us to interchange the limit and the integration under their respective assumptions. The Dominated Convergence Theorem applies to sequences dominated by an integrable function, while the Bounded Convergence Theorem applies, under its additional assumptions such as finite measure and uniform boundedness, to another class of sequences. These classes of sequences overlap, but neither theorem simply contains all the sequences covered by the other. So it is better not to think of them as a simple chain such as: \[ \text{D.C.T} \supset \text{B.C.T} \supset \text{M.C.T}. \] Instead, we should think of these theorems as giving different conditions under which we are allowed to interchange a limit and an integral.

Nevertheless, we won't bother talking about B.C.T and D.C.T. We will talk about them in the next lesson. What we are going to do now is prove additivity of integration, but to do that we need to use M.C.T. But even though in our proof we use the conclusion of M.C.T., it doesn't mean that additivity of integration only works when M.C.T. holds. The only thing we need for the additivity of integration to hold is a valid theorem that allows us to take the limit inside the integration, and as mentioned, the class of sequences of functions for which that is true is much larger than the class of sequences of functions to which M.C.T. applies.

Theorem-5.036

Suppose \((X,\mathcal S, \mu)\) is a measure space and \(f,g: X \to [0,\infty]\) are \(\mathcal S\)-measurable functions. Then: \[ \int (f+g) \, \text{d}\mu = \int f \, \text{d}\mu + \int g \, \text{d}\mu \]

Proof:

Since \(f\) and \(g\) are non-negative measurable functions, we can choose increasing sequences of non-negative simple functions \(f_1,f_2,\dots\) and \(g_1,g_2,\dots\) such that: \[ 0\le f_1(x)\le f_2(x)\le \dots \le f(x), \quad f_k(x)\to f(x) \] and \[ 0\le g_1(x)\le g_2(x)\le \dots \le g(x), \quad g_k(x)\to g(x) \] for every \(x\in X\). Then: \[ f_k(x)+g_k(x)\to f(x)+g(x) \] and, since both sequences are increasing, we also have: \[ 0\le f_1(x)+g_1(x)\le f_2(x)+g_2(x)\le \dots \le f(x)+g(x). \] Therefore, by the M.C.T.: \[ \int (f+g) \, \text{d}\mu = \lim_{k\to\infty}\int (f_k+g_k)\,\text{d}\mu. \]

We have seen that the sum of two \(\mathcal S\)-measurable functions is \(\mathcal S\)-measurable, and \(f_k\) and \(g_k\) are simple functions. Therefore \(f_k+g_k\) is also a simple function. From the theorem we proved above for simple functions, we have: \[ \int (f_k+g_k)\,\text{d}\mu = \int f_k\,\text{d}\mu+\int g_k\,\text{d}\mu. \] Hence: \[ \implies \int (f+g)\,\text{d}\mu = \lim_{k\to\infty} \left( \int f_k\,\text{d}\mu+\int g_k\,\text{d}\mu \right). \] Since both sequences \(\left\{\int f_k\,\text{d}\mu\right\}\) and \(\left\{\int g_k\,\text{d}\mu\right\}\) are increasing, we can take their limits: \[ \implies \int (f+g)\,\text{d}\mu = \lim_{k\to\infty}\int f_k\,\text{d}\mu + \lim_{k\to\infty}\int g_k\,\text{d}\mu. \] And from M.C.T. we get: \[ \lim_{k\to\infty}\int f_k\,\text{d}\mu = \int f\,\text{d}\mu, \qquad \lim_{k\to\infty}\int g_k\,\text{d}\mu = \int g\,\text{d}\mu. \] Therefore: \[ \int (f+g)\,\text{d}\mu = \int f\,\text{d}\mu + \int g\,\text{d}\mu. \] Therefore, \[ \int (f+g)\,\text{d}\mu = \int f\,\text{d}\mu + \int g\,\text{d}\mu. \]

Now we have one of the important properties of integration that we desired. It proves that for all measurable functions that are non-negative, the additivity of integration holds for the Lebesgue integral. Even though in the case of the Riemann integral the additivity does not hold even for some bounded non-negative functions.

So far we have talked about Lebesgue integration of only non-negative functions. Now we will generalize everything we have seen thus far for extended real-valued measurable functions.

Integration of Real-Valued Functions

All this time we have only dealt with functions that are non-negative and hence taken their integral. Here we need to deal with extended real-valued measurable functions \(f: X \to [-\infty,\infty]\). Good thing for us, we don't need to find some new approach to Lebesgue integration. We just need an intuitive approach to represent a real-valued function as a combination of non-negative functions.

And thus the following definition formalizes the notation and approach.

Definition: Suppose \(f: X \to [-\infty, \infty]\) is a function. Then we define the functions \(f^+\) and \(f^-\) from \(X\) to \([0,\infty]\) as: \[ f^+(x)= \begin{cases} f(x) & \text{if } f(x)\geq 0,\\ 0 & \text{if } f(x)\lt 0, \end{cases} \] \[ f^-(x)= \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } f(x)\geq 0,\\ -f(x) & \text{if } f(x)\lt 0. \end{cases} \] Here: \[ f = f^+ - f^- \quad \text{and} \quad |f| = f^+ + f^- \]

The idea behind why we define these functions is quite intuitive to understand. We only know how to take Lebesgue integration of non-negative functions using the tools we have built so far. So we decompose the extended real-valued function \(f: X \to [-\infty,\infty]\) into two parts. One is \(f^+ : X \to [0,\infty]\), which is positive and takes the values of \(f(x)\) when \(f(x)\geq0\), and gives \(0\) when \(f(x)\lt 0\). And for \(f^- : X \to [0,\infty]\), it takes all the \(x\in X\) such that \(f(x)\lt 0\), and multiplies those negative values by \(-1\) to make the output positive, and gives \(0\) otherwise. Essentially, \(f^-\) takes the negative parts of \(f\) and makes them positive so that we can compute their Lebesgue integration. Then, just as we can write \(f=f^+-f^-\), we can define the Lebesgue integral of \(f\) as the integral of its positive part minus the integral of its negative part. And thus we define the integration of an extended real-valued function as follows:

Definition: Integral of a Real-Valued Function Suppose \((X,\mathcal S, \mu)\) is a measure space and \(f: X \to [-\infty,\infty]\) is an \(\mathcal S\)-measurable function such that \(\int |f| \, \text{d}\mu \lt \infty\), that is, \[ \int f^+ \, \text{d}\mu + \int f^- \, \text{d}\mu \lt \infty. \] Then the Lebesgue integral of \(f\) with respect to \(\mu\) is defined as: \[ \int f \, \text{d}\mu = \int f^+ \, \text{d}\mu - \int f^- \, \text{d}\mu \]

Now that we have defined integration of \(\mathcal S\)-measurable real-valued functions, let us prove that indeed Lebesgue integration is linear.

Linearity of Lebesgue Integration

Theorem-5.037: Homogeneity of Integration

Suppose \((X,\mathcal S, \mu)\) is a measure space and \(f: X \to [-\infty, \infty]\) is an \(\mathcal S\)-measurable function such that \(\int |f| \, \text{d}\mu \lt \infty\). Then if \(c \in \mathbb R\), we have: \[ \int cf \, \text{d}\mu = c\int f \, \text{d}\mu \]

Proof:

Let us consider first the case where \(f\) is non-negative and \(c \ge 0\). Then, for some particular \(\mathcal S\)-partition \(P\), we get: \[ \mathcal L(cf , P) = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \mu(A_i) \inf_{A_i} (cf) = c\sum_{i = 1}^{n} \mu(A_i) \inf_{A_i} (f) = c\mathcal L(f , P). \] Taking the supremum over all \(\mathcal S\)-partitions, we get: \[ \int cf \, \text{d}\mu = \sup_{P} \mathcal L(cf , P) = \sup_{P} c\mathcal L(f , P) = c \sup_{P} \mathcal L(f , P) = c \int f \, \text{d}\mu. \]


Now let us consider the case where \(f\) is an extended real-valued function and \(c > 0\). Since we just saw in the first case for non-negative functions that \(\int cf \, \text{d}\mu = c\int f \, \text{d}\mu\), and here the same result applies to \(f^+\) and \(f^-\), we get: \[ \int cf \, \text{d}\mu = \int (cf)^+ \, \text{d}\mu - \int (cf)^- \, \text{d}\mu = \int cf^+ \, \text{d}\mu - \int cf^- \, \text{d}\mu. \] Therefore: \[ \int cf \, \text{d}\mu = c\int f^+ \, \text{d}\mu - c\int f^- \, \text{d}\mu = c\left(\int f^+ \, \text{d}\mu - \int f^- \, \text{d}\mu\right) = c \int f \, \text{d}\mu. \] And therefore we have for an extended real-valued measurable function \(f\) and \(c \gt 0\): \[ \int cf \, \text{d}\mu = c \int f \, \text{d}\mu. \]


Now let us consider the case where \(c \lt 0\). Since \(c \lt 0 \implies -c \gt 0\), we have: \[ (cf)^+ = (-c)f^- \qquad\text{and}\qquad (cf)^- = (-c)f^+. \] Then we get: \[ \int cf \, \text{d}\mu = \int (-c)f^- \, \text{d}\mu - \int (-c)f^+ \, \text{d}\mu \] \[ = (-c)\int f^- \, \text{d}\mu - (-c)\int f^+ \, \text{d}\mu = (-c)\left(\int f^- \, \text{d}\mu - \int f^+ \, \text{d}\mu\right) \] \[ = c\left(\int f^+ \, \text{d}\mu - \int f^- \, \text{d}\mu\right) = c \int f \, \text{d}\mu. \] Finally, when \(c=0\), we trivially have: \[ \int 0f \, \text{d}\mu = 0 = 0\int f \, \text{d}\mu. \] Therefore, for all \(c \in \mathbb R\) and every measurable \(f\) such that \(\int |f| \, \text{d}\mu \lt \infty\), we get that Lebesgue integration obeys homogeneity: \[ \int cf \, \text{d}\mu = c \int f \, \text{d}\mu. \]

Theorem-5.038: Additivity of Integration

Suppose \((X,\mathcal S, \mu)\) is a measure space and \(f: X \to [-\infty, \infty]\) and \(g: X \to [-\infty, \infty]\) are \(\mathcal S\)-measurable functions such that \(\int |f| \, \text{d}\mu \lt \infty\) and \(\int |g| \, \text{d}\mu \lt \infty\). Then: \[ \int (f+g) \, \text{d}\mu = \int f \, \text{d}\mu + \int g \, \text{d}\mu \]

Proof:

Let us write: \[ (f+g)^+-(f+g)^- = f+g = f^+-f^-+g^+-g^-. \] If we now rearrange the terms from both sides, we get: \[ (f+g)^+ + f^- + g^- = (f+g)^- + f^+ + g^+. \] Here all terms on both sides are non-negative, so we can use our Theorem-5.0## to apply additivity: \[ \int (f+g)^+ \, \text{d}\mu + \int f^- \, \text{d}\mu + \int g^- \, \text{d}\mu = \int (f+g)^- \, \text{d}\mu + \int f^+ \, \text{d}\mu + \int g^+ \, \text{d}\mu. \] \[ \implies \int (f+g)^+ \, \text{d}\mu - \int (f+g)^- \, \text{d}\mu = \int f^+ \, \text{d}\mu - \int f^- \, \text{d}\mu + \int g^+ \, \text{d}\mu - \int g^- \, \text{d}\mu. \] Since \[ (f+g)^+ \leq f^+ + g^+ \qquad\text{and}\qquad (f+g)^- \leq f^- + g^-, \] and we already have \[ \int |f| \, \text{d}\mu \lt \infty \qquad\text{and}\qquad \int |g| \, \text{d}\mu \lt \infty, \] all the integrals in the equation above are finite. Therefore: \[ \int (f+g) \, \text{d}\mu = \int f \, \text{d}\mu + \int g \, \text{d}\mu. \] And therefore we have, for measurable functions \(f\) and \(g\) such that \(\int |f| \, \text{d}\mu \lt \infty\) and \(\int |g| \, \text{d}\mu \lt \infty\), that Lebesgue integration obeys additivity: \[ \int (f+g) \, \text{d}\mu = \int f \, \text{d}\mu + \int g \, \text{d}\mu. \]

And now we have proven both additivity and homogeneity of Lebesgue integration, and therefore it is linear.

Now let us just give you two more theorems that are important for analysis.

Theorem-5.039: Lebesgue Integration is Order Preserving

Suppose \((X,\mathcal S, \mu)\) is a measure space and \(f: X \to [-\infty, \infty]\) and \(g: X \to [-\infty, \infty]\) are \(\mathcal S\)-measurable functions such that \(\int |f| \, \text{d} \mu \lt \infty\) and \(\int |g| \, \text{d} \mu \lt \infty\). Then if \(f(x) \le g(x)\) for all \(x \in X\) then: \[ \int f \, \text{d}\mu \le \int g \, \text{d}\mu \]

Proof:

Since \(\int |f| \, \text{d}\mu \lt \infty\) and \(\int |g| \, \text{d}\mu \lt \infty\), both integrals \(\int f \, \text{d}\mu\) and \(\int g \, \text{d}\mu\) are finite and therefore well-defined real numbers. Now, the additivity of the Lebesgue integral tells us that the integral of a sum of two functions is the sum of their integrals, and the homogeneity property — applied with the constant \(c = -1\) — tells us that scaling a function by \(-1\) scales its integral by \(-1\) as well. Putting these two properties together, we can write: \[ \int g \, \text{d}\mu - \int f \, \text{d}\mu = \int (g - f) \, \text{d}\mu \] The reason why this is useful is that now we only need to say something about the sign of \(\int (g-f) \, \text{d}\mu\). Since \(f(x) \le g(x)\) holds for every \(x \in X\) by assumption, it follows that \(g(x) - f(x) \ge 0\) for every \(x \in X\). Since the function \(g - f\) is everywhere non-negative, its Lebesgue integral must also be non-negative, and therefore: \[ \int (g - f) \, \text{d}\mu \ge 0 \] Hence \(\int g \, \text{d}\mu - \int f \, \text{d}\mu \ge 0\), from which we can conclude that: \[ \int f \, \text{d}\mu \le \int g \, \text{d}\mu \]

Theorem-5.040

Suppose \((X,\mathcal S, \mu)\) is a measure space and \(f: X \to [-\infty, \infty]\) is an \(\mathcal S\)-measurable function such that \(\int |f| \, \text{d}\mu \lt \infty\). Then: \[ \left| \int f \, \text{d}\mu \right| \le \int |f| \, \text{d}\mu \]

Proof:

We know that: \[ f = f^+ - f^- \qquad \text{and} \qquad |f| = f^+ + f^-. \] Therefore: \[ \left| \int f \, \text{d}\mu \right| = \left| \int f^+ \, \text{d}\mu - \int f^- \, \text{d}\mu \right| \le \int f^+ \, \text{d}\mu + \int f^- \, \text{d}\mu = \int (f^+ + f^-) \, \text{d}\mu = \int |f| \, \text{d}\mu. \] Therefore: \[ \left| \int f \, \text{d}\mu \right| \le \int |f| \, \text{d}\mu. \]
