Introduction

In this lesson, we will mainly discuss Lebesgue measure. We have seen that the outer measure is not a measure on all subsets of \(\mathbb{R}\), as it violates the third property, and the most desirable property of a measure, which is countable additivity. In this lesson, we will show that the outer measure is a measure when restricted to the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal B\) on \((\mathbb R,\mathcal B)\), and that the outer measure restricted to the borel \(\sigma\)-algebra gives the Lebesgue measure. Although the notion of Lebesgue measure is not restricted to Borel sets of \(\mathbb R\), as we will see in the lesson on Product Measure, the idea of Lebesgue measure extends naturally to \(\mathbb R^n\) and, in a more general setting, to smooth manifolds embedded in \(\mathbb R^n\), using tools such as charts and appropriate notions of induced measure. Said all this, we will introduce Lebesgue measure here on \(\mathbb R\). Later, you will see that extending the idea to much larger spaces will not be much more difficult.

Outer Measure is a Measure on Borel Sets

In this section, we will prove that the outer measure is indeed a measure when it is restricted to the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra, that is, we will prove that countable additivity holds for Borel sets. This proof is not going to be a direct one. Before proving that countable additivity holds for the outer measure on Borel sets, we need to prove some other properties of the outer measure when applied to Borel sets. Below, the proofs build upon one another, and the proof of each theorem should give you better insights into outer measure , \(\sigma\)-algebra, and Borel sets.

Theorem-5.015

Suppose \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint subsets of \(\mathbb R\), and \(B\) is open then: \[ |A \cup B| = |A| + |B| \]

Proof:

In the case when \(|B| = \infty\) then both sides of \(|A \cup B| = |A| + |B|\) become equal to \(\infty\), so now assume that \(|B| < \infty\), then subadditivity tells us that \(|A \cup B| \le |A| + |B|\). So in order to prove that \(|A \cup B| = |A| + |B|\) we need to prove the reverse inequality, that is \(|A \cup B| \ge |A| + |B|\). We proceed proving \(|A \cup B| \ge |A| + |B|\) by first assuming that \(B\) is an open interval then later we will generalize it to all open sets of \(\mathbb R\).

Assume \(B = (a,b)\) for \(a,b \in \mathbb R\) and \(a < b\). We can assume that \(a,b \notin A\), because changing a set by at most two points does not change its outer measure. Let \(I_1,I_2,\dots\) be the sequence of open intervals whose union contains \(A \cup B\). Now for each \(n \in \mathbb Z^{+}\), let us define the following sets: \[ J_n = I_n \cap (-\infty, a) \] Here each \(J_n\) tells us what elements of \(A\) are in \(I_n\) on the interval \((-\infty, a)\), and since some \(I_n\) might contain extra elements other than elements of \(A\), so will \(J_n\). \[ K_n = I_n \cap (a, b) \] Here each \(K_n\) will only contain elements of \(B\), and the union of all \(K_n\) will contain \(B\). \[ L_n = I_n \cap (b,\infty) \] Here each \(L_n\) will contain elements of \(A\) that are in \(I_n\) on the interval \((b,\infty)\), and some other elements.

Now let us think about \(K_n\) and \(L_n\) only, since \(B\) and \(A\) are disjoint and \(K_n\) is only a cover of \(B\), then we can say the union of \(L_n\) and \(J_n\) will contain all the elements of \(A\) and some more, hence: \[ A \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} (J_n \cup L_n) \] Theorem-5.003 then implies: \[ |A| \le \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (\ell(J_n) + \ell(L_n)) \] Similarly for \(B\): \[ B \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} K_n \implies |B| \le \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \ell(K_n) \] Now if we add \(|A|\) and \(|B|\) together we can say: \[ |A| + |B| \le \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (\ell(J_n) + \ell(L_n)) + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \ell(K_n) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \ell(I_n) \] Which is the same as: \[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \ell(I_n) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} (\ell(J_n) + \ell(L_n)) + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \ell(K_n) \ge |A| + |B| \] Notice we choose the cover of \(A \cup B\) to be arbitrary, hence \(I_n\) are arbitrary and thus \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \ell(I_n) \ge |A| + |B|\) is true for all collections of \(I_n\)'s whose union contains \(A \cup B\), therefore we can say: \[ |A \cup B| \ge |A| + |B| \] Hence completing the proof for the case when \(B\) is an open interval: \[ |A \cup B| = |A| + |B| \]

Now consider the case where \(B\) is a union of open intervals that are disjoint from \(A\), meaning: \[ B = I_1 \cup I_2 \cup \dots \cup I_n \] where \(I_n\) are pairwise disjoint open intervals, and \(n \in \mathbb N\), then we can apply induction on \(n\) with the base case \(n=1\) and induction hypothesis \[ \boxed{\text{If } B = I_1 \cup I_2 \cup \dots \cup I_n,\text{ then } |A \cup B| = |A| + |B|.} \] For the induction step, let \(B'=I_1\cup\cdots\cup I_{n-1}\). By the induction hypothesis, \(|A\cup B'|=|A|+|B'|\). Since the \(I_n\)'s are pairwise disjoint and \(I_n\) is disjoint from \(A\), \(I_n\) is disjoint from \(A\cup B'\), so by the open-interval case, \[ |A\cup B|=|A\cup B'\cup I_n| =|A\cup B'|+\ell(I_n) =|A|+|B'|+\ell(I_n) =|A|+|B|. \]

Now assume that \(B\) is an arbitrary open subset of \(\mathbb R\), then: \[ B = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} I_n \] Here \(I_n\) are pairwise disjoint open intervals of \(\mathbb R\) and are disjoint from \(A\). Then: \[ |A \cup B| \ge \left| A \cup \left( \bigcup_{n=1}^{m} I_n\right)\right| = |A| + \sum_{n=1}^{m} \ell(I_n) \] Then: \[ |A \cup B| \ge |A| + \lim_{m\to\infty} \sum_{n=1}^{m} \ell(I_n) \implies |A \cup B| \ge |A| + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \ell(I_n) = |A| + |B| \] And so we get for arbitrary open subsets of \(\mathbb R\), \(B \subset \mathbb R\): \[ \boxed{|A \cup B| \ge |A| + |B| \implies |A \cup B| = |A| + |B| } \]

Hence this is the proof of Theorem-5.010.

We now have seen that when \(A\) is arbitrary subset of \(\mathbb R\) and \(B\) is an open subset of \(\mathbb R\), then outer measure on the union of \(A\) and \(B\) obeys \(|A \cup B| = |A| + |B|\). Now we shall look at the case when \(B\) is closed.

Theorem-5.016

Suppose \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint subsets of \(\mathbb R\), and \(B\) is closed then: \[ |A \cup B| = |A| + |B| \]

Proof:

In this proof, like in the proof of 5.010, we need to prove that \(|A \cup B| \ge |A| + |B|\), as the reverse is proven by Theorem-5.003.

Suppose \(I_1, I_2, \dots\) are a sequence of open intervals whose countable union contains \(A \cup B\), \[ A \cup B \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} I_n \] Let us say: \[ G = \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} I_n \] That means \(G\) is an open subset of \(\mathbb R\). Since \(A \cup B \subseteq \bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty} I_n\), we can say \[ A \subseteq G \setminus B \implies |A| \le |G \setminus B| \] Here we can write \(G \setminus B\) as \(G \cap (\mathbb R \setminus B)\), since \(B\) is closed by the definition of closed sets we can say \(\mathbb R \setminus B\) must be open. Also notice that \(B \subseteq G\), so: \[ G = B \cup (G \setminus B) \] Since \(B\) and \(G \setminus B\) are disjoint and \(G \setminus B\) is open, by Theorem-5.010 we can say: \[ |G| = |B| + |G \setminus B| \] Adding \(|B|\) to \(|A| \le |G \setminus B|\) gives us \[ |A| + |B| \le |G \setminus B| + |B| \implies |A| + |B| \le |G| \] Also, by countable subadditivity: \[ |G| \le \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \ell(I_n) \] Therefore: \[ |A| + |B| \le |G| \le \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \ell(I_n) \] Since the covers were arbitrary covers of \(A \cup B\): \[ |A| + |B| \le |A \cup B| \implies |A \cup B| \ge |A| + |B| \] And so therefore, since we already had \(|A \cup B| \le |A| + |B|\), we now have proved: \[ \boxed{|A \cup B| = |A| + |B| \quad \forall A \subset \mathbb R,\text{ and closed sets } B \subset \mathbb R}. \]

Note, in this Theorem-5.011 \(A\) is arbitrary; it can be closed, open, Borel and even non-Borel set. But we haven't proved yet that there indeed exists such things as sets that are not Borel, and hence the following Theorem-5.013.

Theorem-5.017

Suppose \(B \subset \mathbb R\) is a Borel set, then for every \(\epsilon > 0\) there exists a closed set \(E \subseteq B\) such that: \[ |B \setminus E | \lt \epsilon \]

Proof:

Let us define the collection of sets: \[ \mathcal{L} = \left\{ D \subset \mathbb R : \text{for every } \epsilon>0,\text{ there exists a closed set } F\subseteq D \text{ such that } |D\setminus F|\lt \epsilon \right\}. \] We will show that \(\mathcal{L}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra. Since every closed subset of \(\mathbb R\) belongs to \(\mathcal{L}\), this will show that every Borel set belongs to \(\mathcal{L}\), which proves the theorem. First, let us show that \(\mathcal{L}\) is closed under countable intersections. Suppose \(D_1,D_2,\dots\in\mathcal{L}\) and let \(\epsilon>0\). Since every \(D_k\in\mathcal{L}\), for each \(k\in\mathbb Z^+\) we can choose a closed set \(F_k\subseteq D_k\) such that: \[ |D_k\setminus F_k|\lt\frac{\epsilon}{2^k}. \] Now consider the set: \[ F=\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}F_k. \] Since every \(F_k\) is closed, \(F\) is also closed. Also, \[ F\subseteq\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}D_k. \] On the other hand, if a point belongs to \(\left(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}D_k\right)\setminus F\), then it cannot belong to at least one of the sets \(F_k\). Therefore: \[ \left(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}D_k\right)\setminus F \subseteq \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}(D_k\setminus F_k). \] Hence, by countable subadditivity: \[ \left| \left(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}D_k\right)\setminus F \right| \leq \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}|D_k\setminus F_k| \lt \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\epsilon}{2^k} =\epsilon. \] Thus: \[ \bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}D_k\in\mathcal{L}. \] Therefore, \(\mathcal{L}\) is closed under countable intersections. Now we show that \(\mathcal{L}\) is closed under complements. Suppose \(D\in\mathcal{L}\) and \(\epsilon>0\). First consider the case when \(|D|\lt \infty\). Since \(D\in\mathcal{L}\), we can choose a closed set \(F\subseteq D\) such that: \[ |D\setminus F|\lt \frac{\epsilon}{2}. \] Since \(|D|\lt \infty\), from the definition of outer measure we can also choose an open set \(G\) containing \(D\) such that: \[ |G| \lt |D|+\frac{\epsilon}{2}. \] Let: \[ H=\mathbb R\setminus G. \] Since \(G\) is open, \(H\) is closed. Also, \(D\subseteq G\), so: \[ H\subseteq\mathbb R\setminus D. \] Moreover: \[ (\mathbb R\setminus D)\setminus H = G\setminus D \subseteq G\setminus F. \] Therefore: \[ |(\mathbb R\setminus D)\setminus H| \leq |G\setminus F|. \] Since \(F\) is closed and \(F\subseteq G\), by Theorem-5.011: \[ |G| = |G\setminus F|+|F|. \] So: \[ |G\setminus F| = |G|-|F|. \] Also, because \(D=(D\setminus F)\cup F\), by subadditivity: \[ |D| \leq |D\setminus F|+|F|. \] Hence: \[ |F| \geq |D|-|D\setminus F|. \] Combining these inequalities gives: \[ |G\setminus F| \lt \left(|D|+\frac{\epsilon}{2}\right) - \left(|D|-|D\setminus F|\right) = \frac{\epsilon}{2}+|D\setminus F| \lt \epsilon. \] Therefore: \[ |(\mathbb R\setminus D)\setminus H|\lt \epsilon, \] and so: \[ \mathbb R\setminus D\in\mathcal{L}. \] Now consider the case where \(|D|=\infty\). For each \(k\in\mathbb Z^+\), define: \[ D_k=D\cap[-k,k]. \] Since \(D\in\mathcal{L}\) and \([-k,k]\) is closed, we have \(D_k\in\mathcal{L}\). Also, each \(D_k\) is bounded, and therefore \(|D_k|<\infty\). By the case we have already proved: \[ \mathbb R\setminus D_k\in\mathcal{L}. \] Notice that: \[ D=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}D_k. \] Taking complements gives: \[ \mathbb R\setminus D = \bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty}(\mathbb R\setminus D_k). \] Since we have already shown that \(\mathcal{L}\) is closed under countable intersections, it follows that: \[ \mathbb R\setminus D\in\mathcal{L}. \] Thus, \(\mathcal{L}\) is closed under complements as well. Finally, every closed set \(C\subseteq\mathbb R\) belongs to \(\mathcal{L}\), because for any \(\epsilon>0\) we can simply choose \(F=C\). Then: \[ |C\setminus F|=|C\setminus C|=0 \lt \epsilon. \] Therefore, \(\mathcal{L}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra containing every closed subset of \(\mathbb R\). Since the Borel sets are generated by the closed sets, every Borel set belongs to \(\mathcal{L}\). Hence, for every Borel set \(B\subset\mathbb R\) and every \(\epsilon>0\), there exists a closed set \(E\subseteq B\) such that: \[ \boxed{|B\setminus E|\lt epsilon}. \]

Theorem-5.018

Suppose \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint subsets of \(\mathbb R\) and \(B\) is a Borel set, then: \[ |A \cup B| = |A| + |B| \]

Proof:

In the case when \(|B|=\infty\), since \(B\subseteq A\cup B\), we immediately have \[ |A\cup B|=\infty=|A|+|B|. \] So now assume that \(|B|<\infty\). From Theorem-5.012 we have seen for the Borel set \(B\), for every \(\epsilon \gt 0\), we can find a closed set call it \(E \subseteq B\) such that: \[ |B \setminus E| < \epsilon. \] So since \(E \subseteq B\) and \(A\) and \(B\) are disjoint, we can write: \[ |A \cup B| \ge |A \cup E| = |A| + |E| \] where the equality follows from Theorem-5.011. Also, since \[ B = E \cup (B\setminus E), \] by subadditivity we have: \[ |B| \le |E| + |B\setminus E| \] and therefore: \[ |E| \ge |B| - |B\setminus E| > |B|-\epsilon. \] Thus: \[ |A \cup B| \ge |A|+|E| > |A|+|B|-\epsilon. \] Since \(\epsilon\) is arbitrarily small, we have: \[ |A \cup B| \ge |A| + |B|. \] Like before we already know \(|A \cup B| \le |A| + |B|\), thus we have: \[ |A \cup B| = |A| + |B|. \]

Theorem-5.019

There exists a set \(E \subset \mathbb R\) such that \(|E| < \infty\) and \(E\) is not a Borel set.

Proof:

We have seen in Theorem-507 that there exists disjoint subsets of \(\mathbb R\), let them be \(A\) and \(B\), such that \(|B| < \infty\) and \[ |A \cup B| \neq |A| + |B|. \] But we know from Theorem-5.013 that, for disjoint subsets of \(\mathbb R\), let them be \(A\) and \(B\), and \(B\) is a Borel set and \(|B| < \infty\): \[ |A \cup B| = |A| + |B|. \] So it can't be that \(|A \cup B| \neq |A| + |B|\) can follow if \(B\) is Borel, so there has to be sets that are not Borel, as Theorem-507 proves there indeed are sets such that \[ |A \cup B| \neq |A| + |B|, \] and hence there are subsets of \(\mathbb R\) that are not Borel sets.

Theorem-5.020

Outer measure is a measure on the measure space \((\mathbb R, \mathcal B)\), where \(\mathcal B\) is the \(\sigma\)-algebra of Borel subsets of \(\mathbb R\).

Proof:

Assume we have a sequence of Borel subsets of \(\mathbb R\), \(B_1, B_2, \dots\), where each \(B_k\) is pairwise disjoint. Then for \(n \ge 1\) we can say: \[ \left| \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} B_k \right| \ge \left| \bigcup_{k=1}^{n} B_k \right| \] From Theorem-5.013 we can say: \[ \left| \bigcup_{k=1}^{n} B_k \right| = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left|B_k \right| \implies \left| \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} B_k \right| \ge \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left|B_k \right| \] Now if we take the limit \(n \to \infty\) then we get: \[ \left| \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} B_k \right| \ge \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \left|B_k \right| \implies \left| \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} B_k \right| \ge \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left|B_k \right| \] And from countable subadditivity of outer measure we have: \[ \left| \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} B_k \right| \le \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left|B_k \right| \] Since we have proven both inequalities, we can say outer measure on Borel sets obeys countable additivity \[ \left| \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} B_k \right| = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \left|B_k \right|. \] Therefore we have proven outer measure restricted to \((\mathbb R, \mathcal B)\) is a measure.

As promised, we have shown that outer measure is indeed a measure when restricted to \((\mathbb R, \mathcal B)\), and we also said that when outer measure is restricted to \((\mathbb R, \mathcal B)\), we call it a Lebesgue measure.

In upcoming parts we will formally define Lebesgue measure and prove some of its properties, then we will talk about functions being Lebesgue measurable.

Lebesgue Measure & Lebesgue Measurable Sets

DEFINITION: Lebesgue measure Lebesgue measure is a measure on the measurable space \((\mathbb{R},\mathcal{B})\), where \(\mathcal{B}\) is the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra of \(\mathbb{R}\). It assigns to each Borel set its outer measure.

Even though we defined Lebesgue measure as a measure that assigns outer measure to the Borel sets, we will also see that outer measure is also a measure on a larger class of sets called Lebesgue measurable sets.

DEFINITION: Lebesgue measurable set A set \(A \subset \mathbb R\) is called a Lebesgue measurable set if there exists a Borel set \(B \in \mathcal B\) and \(B \subset A\) such that: \[ |A \setminus B| = 0 \]

The subset of \(\mathbb R\) that are Lebesgue measurable forms a \(\sigma\)-algebra, and it is larger than the Borel \(\sigma\)-algebra. To construct the \(\sigma\)-algebra of Lebesgue measurable sets think of taking all the Borel subsets of \(\mathbb R\), then put them all in the set and call it \(\mathcal L\), then we need to take all the sets \(A\) such that we can find a Borel set \(B\) such that \(|B\setminus A| = 0\), and after putting in all such subsets of \(\mathbb R\), what we will get is the \(\sigma\)-algebra of Lebesgue measurable sets. We will see the proof that indeed it is a \(\sigma\)-algebra, and we will denote that as \(\mathcal L(\mathbb R)\), and the Lebesgue measurable space is \((\mathbb R, \mathcal L(\mathbb R), \mu)\) (Note: later you will see us using \(\lambda\) instead of \(\mu\) to make it explicit that we are working with Lebesgue measure on Lebesgue measurable sets).

There are several definitions of Lebesgue measurable sets, but the definition we used here tells us that a Lebesgue measurable set has a measure difference of zero with some Borel set. As you realize, all such definitions are correct. Starting from any definition, we can reach the other definitions as a result of our chosen definition. Below in the theorem we can see what other statements we could have chosen as the definition, and we will prove that choosing any of them as the definition, the others follow as theorems from the chosen definition.

Theorem-5.021

Suppose \(A \subset \mathbb R\). Then the following are equivalent:

  1. \(A\) is a Lebesgue measurable set.
  2. For each \(\epsilon \gt 0\), there exists a closed set \(F\subset A\), with \(|A \setminus F| \lt \epsilon\).
  3. There exists a sequence of closed sets \(F_1,F_2, \dots \subset A\), such that: \[ \left| A \setminus \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} F_k \right| = 0 \]
  4. There exists a Borel set \(B \subset A\) such that \(|A \setminus B| = 0\).
  5. For every \(\epsilon \gt 0\), there exists an open set \(G \supset A\) such that \(|G \setminus A| \lt \epsilon\).
  6. There exists a sequence of open sets \(G_1,G_2,\dots\), and \(A \subset G_k\), such that: \[ \left|\left(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty} G_k\right) \setminus A \right| = 0 \]
  7. There exists a Borel set \(B\), such that \(|B \setminus A| = 0\)

Proof:

To prove that these statements are all equivalent to each other, we need to prove the following chain of implications: \[ (ii) \iff (iii) \iff (iv) \quad \text{ and } \quad (ii) \iff (v) \iff (vi) \iff (vii) \] Here, to prove this implication chain, we don't need to do both-way implications individually; if we just show the forward implication chain \((ii) \implies (iii) \implies (iv)\) and then at last if we show \((iv) \implies (ii)\), the both-way implication chain will be proven, same for the other chain. So therefore we need to prove: \[ (ii) \implies (iii) \implies (iv) \implies (ii)\quad \text{ and } \quad (ii) \implies (v) \implies (vi) \implies (vii) \implies (ii) \]

From Theorem-5.012 we can bring the Lebesgue \(\sigma\)-algebra, which is: \[ \mathcal L = \{ A \subset \mathbb R: \text{ for every } \epsilon \gt 0 , \text{ there exists a closed set } F \subset A \text{ such that } |A \setminus F| \lt \epsilon\} \] Notice this is based on \((ii)\) from the theorem. We also have seen that \(\mathcal L\) contains all Borel subsets of \(\mathbb R\) as well as sets with measure zero. Now we will start showing how the implication chain follows, starting with:

\((ii) \implies (iii):\) Let us start by assuming that \((ii)\) holds, so we can find a sequence of closed subsets \(F_n \subset A\) such that: \[ |A \setminus F_n| \lt \frac{1}{n} \] Also we can say: \[ A \setminus \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} F_k \subset A \setminus F_n \] for each \(n \in \mathbb Z^+\). Therefore we can say: \[ |A \setminus \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} F_k| \le |A \setminus F_n| \le \frac{1}{n} \] Since the above is true for each \(n \in \mathbb Z^+\), it follows that: \[ |A \setminus \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} F_k| = 0 \] which is the result from \((iii)\).

\((iii) \implies (iv):\) The set: \[ B = \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} F_k \] is a countable union of closed subsets of \(\mathbb R\), which means \(B\) is a Borel set. From \((iii)\) we got \(|A \setminus \bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} F_k| = 0\). Now, if we just let the set be as shown above, that immediately follows \((iv)\).

Therefore, \((iii) \implies (iv)\).

\((iv) \implies (ii):\) Let us assume \((iv)\) holds, so there is a Borel set \(B \subset A\) such that \(|A \setminus B| = 0\). Then we can write: \[ A = B \cup (A \setminus B) \] Notice since \(B\) is a Borel set, it belongs to \(\mathcal L\), and because \(|A \setminus B| = 0\), that implies \(A \setminus B \in \mathcal L\). Since \(B\) and \(A \setminus B\) are both in \(\mathcal L\) and \(\mathcal L\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra, we can say \(A \in \mathcal L\). This proves exactly what we expect for a set \(A\) such that \(|A \setminus B| = 0\), according to our definition of a Lebesgue measurable set and \((i)\), thus \((iv)\implies (i)\).

Now we have proven the implication chain: \[ (ii) \iff (iii) \iff (iv) \] Now similarly, let us follow the next implication chain.

\((ii) \implies (v):\) Since \(A \in \mathcal L\) and \(\mathcal L\) is closed under complementation, \(\mathbb R \setminus A \in \mathcal L\). Given \(\epsilon \gt 0\), pick a closed subset \(F \subset \mathbb R \setminus A\) with \(|(\mathbb R \setminus A) \setminus F| \lt \epsilon\). Set \(G = \mathbb R \setminus F\); then \(G\) is open, \(G \supset A\), and: \[ G \setminus A = (\mathbb R \setminus F) \setminus A = (\mathbb R \setminus A) \setminus F \] So, \[ |G \setminus A| \lt \epsilon. \]

\((v) \implies (vi):\) Assuming \((v)\) holds, then for each \(n \in \mathbb Z^+\) we can choose an open \(G_n \supset A\) with \(|G_n \setminus A| \lt \frac{1}{n}\), since: \[ \left(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty} G_k \right) \setminus A \subset G_n \setminus A \implies \left|\left(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty} G_k \right) \setminus A \right| \le |G_n \setminus A| \le \frac{1}{n} \] Since it holds for each \(n \in \mathbb Z^+\) we get: \[ \left|\left(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty} G_k \right) \setminus A \right| = 0 \]

\((vi) \implies (vii):\) Assuming \((vi)\) holds, so there are \(G_n\) for each \(n \in \mathbb Z^+\) such that: \[ \left|\left(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty} G_k \right) \setminus A \right| = 0 \] Since \(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty} G_k\) is a countable intersection of open sets, it follows that \(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty} G_k\) is a Borel set, and hence, if we let the Borel set \(B\) be: \[ B = \bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty} G_k \] then by \((vi)\) we get: \[ \left|\left(\bigcap_{k=1}^{\infty} G_k \right) \setminus A \right| = |B \setminus A| = 0 \] Hence, proving that \((vi) \implies (vii)\).

\((vii) \implies (ii):\) Assuming \((vii)\) holds, we can say there exists a Borel set \(B \supset A\) such that \(|B \setminus A| = 0\). Let us write \(A\) as: \[ A = B \cap (\mathbb R \setminus (B \setminus A)) \] We know \(B\) is Borel, and thus \(B \in \mathcal L\), and since \(|B \setminus A| = 0\), that implies \(B \setminus A \in \mathcal{L}\), so by the definition of a \(\sigma\)-algebra, \(\mathbb R \setminus (B \setminus A) \in \mathcal{L}\), which implies \(A \in \mathcal L\).

Now we have proven the implication chain: \[ (ii) \iff (v) \iff (vi) \iff (vii) \] Finally proving the theorem, that all statements \((i) \to (vii)\) are equivalent.

Now that we have seen the above theorem, it is time for another important step, that is to show that outer measure is a measure on \((\mathbb R, \mathcal L)\). In Theorem-5.012 we have proven that \(\mathcal L\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\mathbb R\), but now we will see outer measure is a measure on all Lebesgue subsets of \(\mathbb R\).

Theorem-5.022

  1. The set \(\mathcal L\) of Lebesgue measurable subsets of \(\mathbb R\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra.
  2. Outer measure is a measure on the measure space \((\mathbb R, \mathcal L)\).

Proof:

\((i):\) We have seen that \(\mathcal L\) is the set that contains sets that satisfy \((ii)\) of Theorem-5.015, and since \((a) \iff (b)\), we can say all the sets in \(\mathcal L\) are measurable. And we have seen in Theorem-5.012 that \(\mathcal L\) is a sigma algebra. Therefore, all this together proves \((i)\)

\((ii):\) Let there be a sequence of disjoint Lebesgue measurable sets \(A_1,A_2,\dots\). We have seen in Theorem-5.015 \((iv)\) there exists a Borel set \(B_k \subset A_k\) with \(|A_k \setminus B_k| = 0\). Since \(B_k \subset A_k\) for each \(k \in \mathbb Z^+\), we can say: \[ \left|\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} A_k \right| \ge \left|\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} B_k \right| \] Since \(A_k\)'s are disjoint, then that implies so are \(B_k\)'s, so we can write \(\left|\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} B_k \right| = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |B_k|\), so: \[ \left|\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} A_k \right| \ge \left|\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} B_k \right| = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |B_k| \] Now notice we can write \(A_k = B_k \cup (A_k \setminus B_k)\), since \(B_k\) and \(A_k\setminus B_k\) are disjoint, we can write: \[ |A_k| \le |B_k| + |A_k \setminus B_k| = |B_k| + 0 = |B_k| \implies |A_k| \le |B_k| \] It is because we already know \(|A_k \setminus B_k| = 0\), and we also know \(|B_k| \le |A_k|\). Therefore: \[ \left|\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} A_k \right| \ge \left|\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} B_k \right| = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |B_k| = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |A_k| \] From countable subadditivity of outer measure we know that: \[ \left|\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} A_k \right| \le \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |A_k| \] And we got: \[ \left|\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} A_k \right| \ge \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |A_k| \] These two together give us: \[ \left|\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty} A_k \right| = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} |A_k| \] This finally proves the countable additivity, and thus proves \((ii)\).

Now we are ready to define what Lebesgue measure means. Notice a set call it \(A\) with outer measure zero belongs to \(\mathcal L\) as \(B = \varnothing \in \mathcal B\), so \(|A \setminus B| = 0\), which implies \(A \in \mathcal L\). The Lebesgue \(\sigma\)-algebra is the smallest sigma algebra that contains all open sets of \(\mathbb R\) and sets with outer measure zero. Therefore we can say \[ \mathcal L = \sigma\left( \{A \subset \mathbb R: |A| = 0 \text{ or } A \in \mathcal B\}\right). \] There are subsets of \(\mathbb R\) that are not Lebesgue measurable like in the case of Borel measure, as outer measure is not finitely additive.

DEFINITION: Lebesgue measure The Lebesgue measure is the measure on \((\mathbb R,\mathcal L)\) that assigns each Lebesgue measurable set its outer measure.

We have established the idea of Lebesgue measure and Lebesgue measurable sets as well as Lebesgue \(\sigma\)-algebra. To some, this lesson may seem too abstract and irrelevant, but remember, Lebesgue measure assigns measure to sets. In this case, we have seen and constructed the foundation of Lebesgue measurability of subsets of \(\mathbb R\). We have also seen there are sets that are not simply Lebesgue measurable. In the proceeding lesson on product measure, we will see how Lebesgue measure generalizes to \(\mathbb R^n\), and we will see an example that shows how Lebesgue measure gives us the volume of a hypersphere in \(n\) dimensions. Lebesgue measure is going to be used to define Lebesgue integration of functions, as you may remember in the case of the Riemann integral we had to find the length of the interval \(b-a\) as \(b\) and \(a\) get closer to each other, in other words \(\delta x \to 0\), and we multiply \(\delta x\) with \(f(x)\) to get the rectangle's area and sum them up, in the case of Lebesgue measure we use Lebesgue measure that would be different as it generalizes to larger sets than just intervals.
