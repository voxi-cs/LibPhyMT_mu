Lagrange\Libration Equilibrium Points

Imagine you are given the task to build a space telescope and then launch it — but launch it where? We can't let the telescope roam around in space, mainly for three reasons. First, space telescopes need an unobstructed, stable view of deep space; if the telescope moves unpredictably it will be very hard to control. Second, it wouldn't be smart to let a deep-space telescope drift away from Earth — communication delays would grow and operations would become much harder. And most important of all: think about this — if I tell you to go outside in the morning and take pictures of the stars you’ll surely call me dumb, because the Sun is up and we can't really see the sky. Even if you take out your telescope, the Sun’s brightness makes it too difficult to see anything except the brightest stars or planets. So in space we also need to keep our telescope in a place that's always shaded from the Sun if we want to look for very faint, deep objects and phenomena. For those reasons it's much better to keep the telescope relatively close to Earth and fixed with respect to Earth as Earth orbits the Sun, positioned on the side away from the Sun so sunlight can't hit it directly. Since you’re a very smart individual, you decide to put the telescope at a Lagrange point — called \(\mathbf{L_2}\).

Lagrange points are special positions in the restricted three-body problem where the gravitational pulls of the two primaries and the centrifugal force in the rotating frame exactly balance. A test particle placed at a Lagrange point can remain stationary in the rotating frame — that is, it keeps the same position relative to the two primaries. In the idealized circular restricted three-body problem there are five such points: \(L1\), \(L2\), \(L3\), \(L4\) and \(L5\).

In practice, spacecraft do not sit exactly at the mathematical points: they usually follow halo or Lissajous orbits around \(L1\) or \(L2\) and perform small station-keeping burns to stay nearby. These orbits provide the practical advantages of a Lagrange location while handling the dynamical instabilities that exist at some of the points. In this section we will discuss the ideas behind Lagrange points and their derivation; along the way we will write Python code to simulate different systems.

Positioning the Lagrange Points

Now that we know what Lagrange points are, it would be reasonable for us to figure out where their locations are, and that is what we will be doing here. What we are interested in here is a special solution for the CR3BP: points in space where both the velocity and acceleration of the test particle are zero in the rotating frame. You might already have some idea how we are going to do that — I will guess you want us to take the gradient of the effective potential \(U\) and then set it to zero; mathematically, \(\mathbf{\nabla}U=0\). But before we do so, we would like to simplify our effective potential such that the essential dynamics is preserved. First, since \(n\) can be taken as any fixed value we will set \(n=1\), then we won't have to worry about it. Another thing we would like to do is make the effective potential not depend explicitly on \(x\) and \(y\); instead we want to write the effective potential in terms of \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) (distances from the points \((x,y)\) to primaries). Note: none of this affects the essential dynamics of the system — it is just to simplify the calculation.

We know that: \[ {r_1}^2=(x+\mu_2)^2+y^2+z^2 \quad \text{and} \quad {r_2}^2=(x-\mu_1)^2+y^2+z^2 \] So, \[ \mu_1 r_1^2+\mu_2 r_2^2 =\mu_1\big((x+\mu_2)^2+y^2\big)+\mu_2\big((x-\mu_1)^2+y^2\big) \\ =(\mu_1+\mu_2)(x^2+y^2)+\mu_1\mu_2^2+\mu_2\mu_1^2 \\ =x^2+y^2+\mu_1\mu_2, \] Using the fact that \(\mu_1+\mu_2=1\) and \(\mu_1\mu_2^2+\mu_2\mu_1^2=\mu_1\mu_2(\mu_1+\mu_2)=\mu_1\mu_2\) we get: \begin{equation} \mu_1 r_1^2+\mu_2 r_2^2 = x^2+y^2 + \mu_1\mu_2 \end{equation} We know the effective potential is: \[ U =\left[\frac{n^2}{2}\left(x^2+y^2\right)+\frac{\mu_1}{r_1}+\frac{\mu_2}{r_2}\right] \] Rearranging the term in eq(63) for \(x^2+y^2\) we can rewrite the potential as: \[ U =\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\mu_1 r_1^2+\mu_2 r_2^2 - \mu_1\mu_2 \right)+\frac{\mu_1}{r_1}+\frac{\mu_2}{r_2}\right] \] Rearranging terms gives us: \begin{equation} U = \mu_1\left(\frac{{r_1}^2}{2}+\frac{1}{r_1}\right) + \mu_2\left(\frac{{r_2}^2}{2}+\frac{1}{r_2}\right) - \frac{1}{2} \mu_1 \mu_2 \end{equation} Alright, so the gradient of the effective potential is, \(\mathbf{\nabla}U = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x} \hat{x} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial y} \hat{y} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial z} \hat{z}\right)\). Since \(U\) is independent of \(z\) because we put our primaries on the \(xy\)-plane, the partial derivative of \(U\) with respect to \(z\) is zero, and the partial derivatives of \(U\) with respect to \(x\) and \(y\) are: \[ \frac{\partial U}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial U}{\partial r_1} \frac{\partial r_1}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial r_2} \frac{\partial r_2}{\partial x} = \left[{\mu}_{1} \left(r_{1} - \frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}\right)\right] \left[\frac{x + {\mu}_{2}}{r_1}\right] + \left[{\mu}_{2} \left(r_{2} - \frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}\right)\right]\left[\frac{x - {\mu}_{1}}{r_2}\right] \] And, \[ \frac{\partial U}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial U}{\partial r_1} \frac{\partial r_1}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial r_2} \frac{\partial r_2}{\partial y} =\left[{\mu}_{1} \left(r_{1} - \frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}\right)\right] \left[\frac{y}{r_1}\right] + \left[{\mu}_{2} \left(r_{2} - \frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}\right)\right] \left[\frac{y}{r_2}\right] \] So, after evaluating the partial derivatives we have: \begin{equation} \left[{\mu}_{1} \left(r_{1} - \frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}\right)\right] \left[\frac{x + {\mu}_{2}}{r_1}\right] + \left[{\mu}_{2} \left(r_{2} - \frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}\right)\right]\left[\frac{x - {\mu}_{1}}{r_2}\right] = 0 \end{equation} \begin{equation} \left[{\mu}_{1} \left(r_{1} - \frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}\right)\right] \left[\frac{y}{r_1}\right] + \left[{\mu}_{2} \left(r_{2} - \frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}\right)\right] \left[\frac{y}{r_2}\right]=0 \end{equation}

One solution of this system could be if \(\frac{\partial U}{\partial r_1}\) and \(\frac{\partial U}{\partial r_2}\) are zero, then: \[ \frac{\partial U}{\partial r_1} = \left[{\mu}_{1} \left(r_{1} - \frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}\right)\right] = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{\partial U}{\partial r_2} = \left[{\mu}_{2} \left(r_{2} - \frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}\right)\right]=0 \] And by definition \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) are greater than zero; solving these equations we get: \[ r_1 = r_2 = 1 \] So, using equation (33) and (34) we have the following system of equations: \[ 1=(x+\mu_2)^2+y^2 \quad \text{and} \quad 1=(x-\mu_1)^2+y^2 \] Solving them we get: \[ x=\frac{1}{2} - \mu_2 \quad \text{and} \quad y=\pm \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \] So, we get a solution — a point in space where the gradient of the effective potential is zero. But look carefully: we got one solution for \(x\), while for \(y\) we got two solutions, \(y=+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\) and \(y=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\). This corresponds to two points in space where the gradient of the effective potential is zero, \(\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mu_2,+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)\) and \(\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mu_2,-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)\). What that means is that if a particle of small mass is at these points, the net force on the particle is zero and the particle will stay stationary at those points. These two points have a very special name and we talked about them before: they are known as the \(L4\) and \(L5\) Lagrange points. \(\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mu_2,+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)\) is the \(L4\) and \(\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mu_2,-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)\) is the \(L5\) Lagrange point. Here, although we chose \(a = n = 1\), the essential dynamics are still the same. One thing we can notice is that the triangle formed by connecting the primaries with the points we found is an \(\mathbf{equilateral\ triangle}\). You can prove that using simple middle-school math, so we will leave it up to the reader to show that the triangle formed by the points \((x_1,y_1)=(-\mu_2,0)\), \((x_2,y_2)=(\mu_1,0)\) and \(\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mu_2,\pm\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)\) is equilateral. If we didn't set \(a = n = 1\), the \(L4\) and \(L5\) points will be at \(\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mu_2\right)a,+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a\right)\) and \(\left(\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mu_2\right)a,-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a\right)\) and we dont have to worry about \(n\). Yeah, so we have \(L4\) and \(L5\) are at coordinate: \begin{equation} \boxed{L4: \customquad \left(\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mu_2\right)a,+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a\right)} \end{equation} \begin{equation} \boxed{L5: \customquad \left(\left(\frac{1}{2}-\mu_2\right)a,-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a\right)} \end{equation}

Going back to eq(66), we can see that \(y=0\) is also a solution to the equations. It is likely that somewhere on the \(x\)-axis (where \(y=0\)) there is maybe one or more Lagrange points, but to know for sure we have to find them. (A reminder: \(a\) is the separation between the primaries.) Like previously we will let \(a=1\) and after we find the solution we can just multiply by a to get the Lagrange Point for any CR3BP or any system in approximation of CR3BP. For a point on the \(x\)-axis, the sum of the distances — the magnitudes of the displacement vectors from each primary to that point — equals \(a=1\). What it basically means is that: \[ r_1+r_2=1 \] From this we get: \[ r_1=x+\mu_2,\quad r_2=\mu_1-x \quad \Rightarrow \frac{\partial r_1}{\partial x}=-\frac{\partial r_2}{\partial x}=1 \] Alright, now using this to substitute into eq(65) gives us: \[ \left[{\mu}_{1} \left(r_{1} - \frac{1}{r_{1}^{2}}\right)\right] + \left[{\mu}_{2} \left(r_{2} - \frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}\right)\right](-1) = 0 \] Here \(\frac{x+\mu_2}{r_1}=1\) and \(\frac{x-\mu_1}{r_2}=-1\). \[\Rightarrow \left[{\mu}_{1} \left(1-r_2 - \frac{1}{(1-{r}_{2})^{2}}\right)\right] - \left[{\mu}_{2} \left(r_{2} - \frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}\right)\right] = 0 \] \[\Rightarrow \frac{\mu_2}{\mu_1} = \frac{\left(1-r_2 - \frac{1}{(1-{r}_{2})^{2}}\right)}{\left(r_{2} - \frac{1}{r_{2}^{2}}\right)} \] \[\Rightarrow \frac{\mu_2}{\mu_1} = 3{r}_{2}^{3}\frac{1-r_2+\frac{{r}^{2}_{2}}{3}}{\left(1+r_2+{r}_{2}^{2}\right)\left(1-r_2\right)^3} \] \begin{equation} \frac{\mu_2}{3\mu_1} = {r}_{2}^{3}\frac{1-r_2+\frac{{r}^{2}_{2}}{3}}{\left(1+r_2+{r}_{2}^{2}\right)\left(1-r_2\right)^3} \end{equation} To actually find the Lagrange point we have to solve this equation; unfortunately we can't just use a formula to compute \(r_2\). Instead here we have to find an approximation for \(r_2\); we're going to do that by something called the Lagrange–Bürmann inversion method. To do so first we need to series-expand this: \(\frac{1-r_2+\frac{{r}^{2}_{2}}{3}}{\left(1+r_2+{r}_{2}^{2}\right)\left(1-r_2\right)^3}\). Let, \[ A(r_2) \equiv \frac{1-r_2+\frac{{r}^{2}_{2}}{3}}{\left(1+r_2+{r}_{2}^{2}\right)\left(1-r_2\right)^3}, \quad Q(r_2) \equiv \left(1+r_2+{r}_{2}^{2}\right)\left(1-r_2\right)^3 \] So, First expanding \(Q(r_2)\) gives us: \[ Q(r_2) = 1-3{r}_{2}+3{r}_{2}^{2}-{r}_{2}^{3}+{r}_{2}-3{r}_{2}^{2}+3{r}_{2}^{3}-{r}_{2}^{4}+{r}_{2}^{2}-3{r}_{2}^{3}+3{r}_{2}^{4}-{r}_{2}^{5} \] \[\Rightarrow Q(r_2) = 1-2{r}_{2} + {r}^2_{2}-{r}_{2}^{3}+2{r}_{2}^{4}-{r}_{2}^{5} \] For small \(r_2\) \[ Q(r_2) = 1-2{r}_{2} + {r}^2_{2}-{r}_{2}^{3}+2{r}_{2}^{4}+\mathcal{O}(r^5) \] We want \(E(r_2)=1/Q(r_2)\), where \(E(r_2)\) is something like below: \[ E(r_2) = \frac{1}{Q(r_2)} = 1 + e_1{r}_{2}+e_2{r}^{2}_{2}+e_3{r}^{3}_{2}+e_4{r}^{4}_{2} +... \] If we enforce \(Q(r_2)E(r_2)=1\): \[ \left(1-2{r}_{2} + {r}^2_{2}-{r}_{2}^{3}+2{r}_{2}^{4}-{r}_{2}^{5}\right)\left(1 + e_1{r}_{2}+e_2{r}^{2}_{2}+e_3{r}^{3}_{2}+e_4{r}^{4}_{2} +...\right)=1 \] Here, \(Q(r_2)=\sum_{\substack{i=0}}^{\infty}d_i {r}_2^{i}\) and \(E(r_2)=\sum_{\substack{i=0}}^{\infty}e_i {r}_2^{i}\), so: \[ Q(r_2)E(r_2)=\sum_{\substack{i=0}}^{\infty}d_i {r}_2^{i}\sum_{\substack{i=0}}^{\infty}e_i {r}_2^{i}=1 \] Using the Cauchy product rule for sums we get: \[ \sum_{\substack{i=0}}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{\substack{j=0}}^{i}d_j e_{i-j}\right){r}_{2}^{i}=1 \] For \(Q(r_2)E(r_2)=1\) to hold, all coefficients of positive powers of \(r_2\) must vanish. That means for \(i\geq 1\), \[ \sum_{\substack{j=0}}^{i}d_j e_{i-j}=0 \] Now that we have this, we can compute the coefficients of \(E(r_2)\), the \(e_i\)'s, so: \[ e_i = -\left(d_i+ \sum_{j=1}^{i-1} d_j e_{i-j}\right) \] So, using this recurrence we get: \[ E(r_2) = 1 + 2{r}_{2}+3{r}^{2}_{2}+5{r}^{3}_{2}+7{r}^{4}_{2} + 9{r}^{5}_{2}+12{r}^{6}_{2}+15{r}^{7}_{2}+18{r}^{8}_{2}+22{r}^{9}_{2}+26{r}^{10}_{2}+30{r}^{11}_{2}+35{r}^{12}_{2}+40{r}^{13}_{2}+45{r}^{14}_{2}+51{r}^{15}_{2}+... \] So, we can write for small \(r_2\): \[ E(r_2) = 1 + 2{r}_{2}+3{r}^{2}_{2}+5{r}^{3}_{2}+7{r}^{4}_{2} + \mathcal{O}(r^5) \] If we now use \(E(r_2)\) and substitute the result we get: \[ A(r_2) = \left(1 - r_2 + \frac{r_2^{2}}{3}\right) E(r_2) \] \[ A(r_2) = \biggl(1 - r_2 + \frac{r_2^{2}}{3}\biggr) \biggl( 1 + 2 r_2 + 3 r_2^{2} + 5 r_2^{3} + 7 r_2^{4} + 9 r_2^{5} + 12 r_2^{6} + 15 r_2^{7} + 18 r_2^{8} + 22 r_2^{9} + 26 r_2^{10} + 30 r_2^{11} + 35 r_2^{12} + \dots \biggr) \]

We also want to series-expand \(A(r_2)\), so if we multiply \(\biggl(1 - r_2 + \frac{r_2^{2}}{3}\biggr)\) with \(E(r_2)\) then we get: \[ A(r_2)=1*\left(1 + 2 r_2 + 3 r_2^{2} + 5 r_2^{3} + 7 r_2^{4} + 9 r_2^{5} + 12 r_2^{6} + 15 r_2^{7} + 18 r_2^{8} + 22 r_2^{9} + 26 r_2^{10} + 30 r_2^{11} + 35 r_2^{12} + 40 r_2^{13} + 45 r_2^{14} + 51 r_2^{15} + \dots\right) \] \[ -r_2\left(1 + 2 r_2 + 3 r_2^{2} + 5 r_2^{3} + 7 r_2^{4} + 9 r_2^{5} + 12 r_2^{6} + 15 r_2^{7} + 18 r_2^{8} + 22 r_2^{9} + 26 r_2^{10} + 30 r_2^{11} + 35 r_2^{12} + 40 r_2^{13} + 45 r_2^{14} + 51 r_2^{15} + \dots\right) \] \[ +\frac{1}{3}r_{2}^{2}\left(1 + 2 r_2 + 3 r_2^{2} + 5 r_2^{3} + 7 r_2^{4} + 9 r_2^{5} + 12 r_2^{6} + 15 r_2^{7} + 18 r_2^{8} + 22 r_2^{9} + 26 r_2^{10} + 30 r_2^{11} + 35 r_2^{12} + 40 r_2^{13} + 45 r_2^{14} + 51 r_2^{15} + \dots\right) \] So, we get: \begin{equation} A(r_2)=\left(1 + 2 r_2 + 3 r_2^{2} + 5 r_2^{3} + 7 r_2^{4} + 9 r_2^{5} + 12 r_2^{6} + 15 r_2^{7} + 18 r_2^{8} + 22 r_2^{9} + 26 r_2^{10} + 30 r_2^{11} + 35 r_2^{12} + 40 r_2^{13} + 45 r_2^{14} + 51 r_2^{15} + \dots\right) \end{equation} \[ +\left(-r_2 - 2 r_{2}^{2} - 3 r_2^{3} - 5 r_2^{4} - 7 r_2^{5} - 9 r_2^{6} - 12 r_2^{7} - 15 r_2^{8} - 18 r_2^{9} - 22 r_2^{10} - 26 r_2^{11} - 30 r_2^{12} - 35 r_2^{13} - 40 r_2^{14} - 45 r_2^{15} - 51 r_2^{16} - \dots\right) \] \[ +\left(\frac{1}{3}r_{2}^{2} + \frac{2}{3}r_{2}^{3} + r_2^{4} + \frac{5}{3} r_2^{5} + \frac{7}{3} r_2^{6} + 3 r_2^{7} + 4 r_2^{8} + 5 r_2^{9} + 6 r_2^{10} + \frac{22}{3} r_2^{11} + \frac{26}{3} r_2^{12} + 10 r_2^{13} + \frac{35}{3} r_2^{14} + \frac{40}{3} r_2^{15} + 15 r_2^{16} + 17 r_2^{17} + \dots\right) \] Since we series-expand \(A(r_2)\) we need an expression of \(A(r_2)\) like below: \[ A(r_2) = a_0+a_1{r}_{2}^{1}+a_2{r}_{2}^{2}+a_3{r}_{2}^{3}+\dots \] So, we can say \(1=a_0\), \(r_{2}\left(2-1\right) = a_1r_2\) and so on; by matching each degree in \(A(r_2)\) with the terms in eq(70) we get: \[ A(r_2) = 1 + r_2 + \tfrac{4}{3}r_2^2 + \tfrac{8}{3}r_2^3 + 3r_2^4 + \tfrac{11}{3}r_2^5 + \tfrac{16}{3}r_2^6 + 6r_2^7 + 7r_2^8 + 9r_2^9 + 10r_2^{10} + \tfrac{34}{3}r_2^{11} + \tfrac{41}{3}r_2^{12} + 15r_2^{13} + \tfrac{50}{3}r_2^{14} + \tfrac{58}{3}r_2^{15} + \dots \] Now, let \(u\) be \[ u = r_2 + \tfrac{4}{3}r_2^2 + \tfrac{8}{3}r_2^3 + 3r_2^4 + \tfrac{11}{3}r_2^5 + \tfrac{16}{3}r_2^6 + 6r_2^7 + 7r_2^8 + 9r_2^9 + 10r_2^{10} + \tfrac{34}{3}r_2^{11} + \tfrac{41}{3}r_2^{12} + 15r_2^{13} + \tfrac{50}{3}r_2^{14} + \tfrac{58}{3}r_2^{15} + \dots \]
\[ A(r_2) = 1 + u \] Alright, going back to eq(69) we had: \[ \frac{\mu_2}{3\mu_1} = {r}_{2}^{3}\frac{1-r_2+\frac{{r}^{2}_{2}}{3}}{\left(1+r_2+{r}_{2}^{2}\right)\left(1-r_2\right)^3} \] let's define \(\alpha\) to be: \[ \alpha \equiv \left(\frac{\mu_2}{3\mu_1}\right)^{1/3} \] That means: \[ \alpha = r_2\left(A(r_2)\right)^{1/3}=r_2\left(1+u\right)^{\frac{1}{3}} \] Now, again if we expand \(\left(1+u\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\) using the binomial expansion we get: \[ \left(1+u\right)^{\frac{1}{3}} = 1 + \frac{1}{3} {r}_{2} + \frac{1}{3} {r}_{2}^{2} + \frac{53}{81} {r}_{2}^{3} + \frac{101}{243} {r}_{2}^{4} + \frac{97}{243} {r}_{2}^{5} + \frac{3953}{6561} {r}_{2}^{6} + \frac{8585}{19683} {r}_{2}^{7} + \dots \] So, now if we substitute this into the equation for \(\alpha\) we get: \[ \alpha = r_2 \left(1 + \frac{1}{3} {r}_{2} + \frac{1}{3} {r}_{2}^{2} + \frac{53}{81} {r}_{2}^{3} + \frac{101}{243} {r}_{2}^{4} + \frac{97}{243} {r}_{2}^{5} + \frac{3953}{6561} {r}_{2}^{6} + \frac{8585}{19683} {r}_{2}^{7} + \dots\right) \] \[ \alpha = {r}_{2} + \frac{1}{3} {r}_{2}^{2} + \frac{1}{3} {r}_{2}^{3} + \frac{53}{81} {r}_{2}^{4} + \frac{101}{243} {r}_{2}^{5} + \mathcal{O}({r}_{2}^{6}) \] So, for small \(r_2\) we can ignore the higher order terms. \[ \alpha = {r}_{2} + \frac{1}{3} {r}_{2}^{2} + \frac{1}{3} {r}_{2}^{3} + \frac{53}{81} {r}_{2}^{4} + \frac{101}{243} {r}_{2}^{5} + \mathcal{O}({r}_{2}^{6}) \] But, we need to find \(r_2\) in order to find the Lagrange point, so how do we do it? Like here we have a series of \(r_2\) but from this we need to solve for \(r_2\); as you can see we can't use elementary algebra to solve for \(r_2\) but instead here we are going to use the Lagrange–Bürmann inversion method which says:
Suppose \(z\) is defined as a function of \(x\) of the form, \[ z=\phi(x) \] where \(\phi\) is analytic at a point \(a\) and \(\phi'(a)\neq0\). Then it is possible to solve the equation for \(x\), expressing the form \(x=g(z)\) given by a power series: \begin{equation} g(z) = a+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}g_n \frac{\left(z-\phi(a)\right)^n}{n!} \end{equation} where, \begin{equation} g_n = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{d^{n-1}}{dx^{n-1}} \left[\left(\frac{x-a}{\phi(x)-\phi(a)}\right)^n\right] \end{equation} So, using this method we can find an expression for \(r_2\). Here we will let \(a=0\) since \(r_2\) is small, so we want to expand \(r_2\) around zero. Well, but how do we analyze stability of something? Are there some kind of scale or something like a "stability meter"? Well no — actually, we're not exactly assigning a single value to the stability of the Lagrange points. What we are going to do is something called Linear Stability Analysis, which will allow us to see what happens to the state vector of the particle and how its components change with time if it deviates from the equilibrium position by a small amount. Remember we said before for the collinear Lagrange points: if we start with a tiny displacement from the equilibrium point then the error builds up exponentially — this is what linear stability analysis allows us to see. We will find that for collinear points \((X(t),Y(t))\) the solution is a linear combination of exponentials \(e^{\lambda t}\), if some \(\lambda>0\) those modes grow, so the collinear points are extremely unstable.

To do Linear Stability Analysis first we need to linearize the equations of motion. Let the location of the equilibrium position be \((x_0, y_0)\) and let \((X,Y)\) be a small displacement such that the particle is at \((x,y) = (x_0 + X,\, y_0 + Y)\). Now if we substitute this into equations (41) and (42) then \(\ddot{x} = \ddot{(x_0+X)} = \ddot{X}\) and similarly \(\ddot{y} = \ddot{Y}\). Let \(U_i \equiv \frac{\partial U}{\partial i}\). So equations (41) and (42) become \[ \ddot{X} - 2n\dot{Y} = U_x(x_0+X, y_0+Y) \] \[ \Rightarrow\quad \ddot{X} - 2n\dot{Y} = U_x(x_0,y_0) + X\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\right)\Big|_0 + Y\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\right)\Big|_0 + \mathcal{O}((X,Y)^2). \] Using the standard notation \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\right)=U_{xx}\) and \(\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\right)=U_{xy}\), we get \[ \ddot{X} - 2n\dot{Y} = U_x\big|_0 + XU_{xx}\big|_0 + YU_{xy}\big|_0 + \mathcal{O}((X,Y)^2). \] At equilibrium points \((x_0, y_0)\) the gradient of \(U\) vanishes: \[ U_x\big|_0 = 0,\quad U_y\big|_0 = 0. \] So, making the linear approximation gives \begin{equation} \ddot{X} - 2n\dot{Y} \approx XU_{xx}\big|_0 + YU_{xy}\big|_0 = X\left(\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial x^2}\right)_0 + Y\left(\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial x\partial y}\right)_0. \end{equation} Similarly, \begin{equation} \ddot{Y} + 2n\dot{X} \approx XU_{yx}\big|_0 + YU_{yy}\big|_0 = X\left(\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial x\partial y}\right)_0 + Y\left(\frac{\partial^2 U}{\partial y^2}\right)_0. \end{equation} Here the subscript \(|_0\) means we are evaluating the second partial derivatives at the equilibrium point where \(\left.\frac{\partial U}{\partial x}\right|_0=\left.\frac{\partial U}{\partial y}\right|_0=0\). Because we are interested in small displacements around the equilibrium points it is reasonable to drop the higher-order terms; this will be useful for our calculations. Now we will do our standard procedure to linearise the equations of motion.

We had our eq(85) and eq(86): \[ \ddot{X} - 2n\dot{Y} \approx XU_{xx}\big|_0 + YU_{xy}\big|_0, \] \[ \ddot{Y} + 2n\dot{X} \approx XU_{yx}\big|_0 + YU_{yy}\big|_0. \] We can write our equations in matrix form as shown below: \[ \begin{pmatrix} \dot{X} \\ \dot{Y} \\ \ddot{X} \\ \ddot{Y} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\[4pt] U_{xx} & U_{xy} & 0 & 2n \\[4pt] U_{xy} & U_{yy} & -2n & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} X \\ Y \\ \dot{X} \\ \dot{Y} \end{pmatrix}. \] Now instead of two simultaneous second-order equations we have transformed the problem into solving four simultaneous first-order differential equations. We can (and should) write eq(87) as \begin{equation} \dot{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{X}, \end{equation} where \[ \mathbf{X} = \begin{pmatrix} X \\ Y \\ \dot{X} \\ \dot{Y} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\[4pt] U_{xx} & U_{xy} & 0 & 2n \\[4pt] U_{xy} & U_{yy} & -2n & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \] Here \(\mathbf{A}\) is a \(4\times4\) transformation matrix \(\mathbb{R}^4\to\mathbb{R}^4\), and \(\mathbf{X}\) is a 4-dimensional state vector. In this procedure our first step is to find the eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\) and eigenvectors of the transformation matrix \(\mathbf{A}\). NOTE: Eigenvalues are scalars \(\lambda_i\) such that \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_i=\lambda_i\mathbf{x}_i\) for some nonzero vector \(\mathbf{x}_i\) (the corresponding eigenvector).

Let's take \(n=1\). Then we can write \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}_i=\lambda_i\mathbf{x}_i\) as: \[ \left(\mathbf{A} - \lambda_i \mathbb{I}_4\right) \mathbf{x}_i = 0. \] This implies the determinant of the matrix \(\mathbf{A} - \lambda_i \mathbb{I}_4\) is zero, so \[ \det(\mathbf{A} - \lambda_i \mathbb{I}_4) = 0. \] If we expand this we obtain the characteristic (polynomial) equation; solving that will give us the eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\).

\[ \det(\mathbf{A} - \lambda\mathbf{I}) = 0 = \begin{vmatrix} -\lambda & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -\lambda & 0 & 1 \\ U_{xx} & U_{xy} & -\lambda & 2 \\ U_{xy} & U_{yy} & -2 & -\lambda \end{vmatrix} \] If we calculate the determinant we get: \begin{equation} \lambda^{4} + (4 - U_{xx} - U_{yy})\lambda^{2} + (U_{xx}U_{yy} - U_{xy}^2) = 0. \end{equation} If we solve this we get the following eigenvalues: \begin{equation} \lambda_{1,2} = \pm \left[ \frac{1}{2}(U_{xx} + U_{yy} - 4) - \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{ (4 - U_{xx} - U_{yy})^2 - 4\big(U_{xx}U_{yy} - U_{xy}^2\big) } \right]^{1/2} \end{equation} \begin{equation} \lambda_{3,4} = \pm \left[ \frac{1}{2}(U_{xx} + U_{yy} - 4) + \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{ (4 - U_{xx} - U_{yy})^2 - 4\big(U_{xx}U_{yy} - U_{xy}^2\big) } \right]^{1/2}. \end{equation} Our eq(87), as you can see, is coupled — that is \(\ddot{X}\) depends on \((X,Y,\dot{Y})\). In this kind of scenario it is useful if we can remove the coupling; this can be done by something called a Similarity / Homothetic transformation. To do this first we let there be a transformation matrix \(\mathbf{B}\) such that we can transform from \(\mathbf{X}\) to some arbitrary vector \(\mathbf{Y}\). Then: \begin{equation} \mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{X}\end{equation} So that implies: \begin{equation}\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{Y} \quad, \dot{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{B}^{-1}\dot{\mathbf{Y}}\end{equation} And so if we now substitute \(\mathbf{X}\) in eq(91) we get: \begin{equation} \dot{\mathbf{Y}} = \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{Y}. \end{equation} Now here is the thing: \(\dot{\mathbf{Y}}\) is equal to some matrix times \(\mathbf{Y}\). The equation is uncoupled when each \(\dot{Y}_i\) depends only on \(Y_i\). One can then say for this to happen the matrix \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\) has to be diagonal. Those who are familiar with eigenvalues and eigenvectors and know a bit of linear algebra can already guess that means \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\) should be the diagonal matrix \(\Lambda\) whose diagonal entries are the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{A}\). Here we choose \(\mathbf{B}\) so that \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\) is the diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{A}\). If you studied linear algebra you know to pick \(\mathbf{B}\) with columns equal to the eigenvectors of \(\mathbf{A}\); if not, make it a homework to show why choosing the eigenvectors of \(\mathbf{A}\) as the columns of \(\mathbf{B}\) makes \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{-1}\) diagonal — hint: use the definition of eigenvalues and eigenvectors. So: \[ \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{-1} = \Lambda = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & \lambda_n \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \lambda_3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \lambda_4 \end{pmatrix}. \] So, now that we have \(\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}^{-1} = \Lambda\), putting it into eq(93) gives: \begin{equation} \dot{\mathbf{Y}} = \Lambda \mathbf{Y}\end{equation} so we can also write this as: \begin{equation}\dot{Y}_i = \lambda_i Y_i\end{equation} We know the general solution to this kind of differential equation: if we have an ODE of the form \(\dot{x} = c x\) then the general solution is \(x(t) = c' e^{ct}\). So we get: \begin{equation} Y_i = c_i e^{\lambda_i t}\quad \text{here, } i =1,2,...,n\end{equation}

Now that we have the solution for \(Y_i\) we can transform this back to \(\mathbf{X}\): \begin{equation} \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{B}^{-1} \begin{pmatrix} c_1 e^{\lambda_1 t} \\ c_2 e^{\lambda_2 t} \\ \vdots \\ c_n e^{\lambda_n t} \end{pmatrix} \end{equation} Eq(97) can be written as: \begin{equation} X = \sum_{j=1}^4 \bar{\alpha}_j e^{\lambda_j t}, \quad \dot{X} = \sum_{j=1}^4 \bar{\alpha}_j \lambda_j e^{\lambda_j t}. \end{equation} Here the \(\bar{\alpha}_j\)'s are constants; similarly for \(Y\) and \(\dot{Y}\): \begin{equation} Y = \sum_{j=1}^4 \bar{\beta}_j e^{\lambda_j t}, \quad \dot{Y} = \sum_{j=1}^4 \bar{\beta}_j \lambda_j e^{\lambda_j t}. \end{equation} The coefficients \(\bar{\beta}_j\) are functions of the \(\bar{\alpha}_j\), and the relation between them can be obtained by substituting the expressions for \(X\), \(Y\) and \(\dot{Y}\) into eq(85): \begin{equation} \sum_{j=1}^4 \left( \bar{\alpha}_j \lambda_j^2 - 2n\bar{\beta}_j \lambda_j - U_{xx}\bar{\alpha}_j - U_{xy}\bar{\beta}_j \right) e^{\lambda_j t} = 0. \end{equation} From this equation we get the following relation: \begin{equation} \bar{\beta}_j = \frac{\lambda_j^2 - U_{xx}}{\,2n\lambda_j + U_{xy}\,}\,\bar{\alpha}_j. \end{equation} Initially, at time \(t=0\), we have the boundary conditions \(X=X_0\), \(Y=Y_0\), \(\dot{X}=\dot{X}_0\) and \(\dot{Y}=\dot{Y}_0\). From these boundary conditions we can determine the four values of \(\bar{\alpha}_j\) from the solution of the four simultaneous linear equations: \begin{equation} \sum_{j=1}^4 \bar{\alpha}_j = X_0,\quad \sum_{j=1}^4 \lambda_j \bar{\alpha}_j = \dot{X}_0,\quad \sum_{j=1}^4 \bar{\beta}_j = Y_0,\quad \sum_{j=1}^4 \lambda_j \bar{\beta}_j = \dot{Y}_0. \end{equation} Although the complete solution is described by Eqs(98) and (99) with the constants \(\bar{\alpha}_j\) and \(\bar{\beta}_j\) given by the solutions to eq(102), the stability of the equilibrium points can be determined from an inspection of the eigenvalues alone. To explore the nature of the eigenvalues we define the quantities: \begin{equation} \bar{A} = \frac{\mu_1}{(r_1^3)_0} + \frac{\mu_2}{(r_2^3)_0}, \end{equation} \begin{equation} \bar{B} = 3\left[\frac{\mu_1}{(r_1^5)_0} + \frac{\mu_2}{(r_2^5)_0}\right] y_0^2, \end{equation} \begin{equation} \bar{C} = 3\left[\mu_1\frac{(x_0 + \mu_2)}{(r_1^5)_0} + \mu_2\frac{(x_0 - \mu_1)}{(r_2^5)_0}\right] y_0, \end{equation} \begin{equation} \bar{D} = 3\left[\mu_1\frac{(x_0 + \mu_2)^2}{(r_1^5)_0} + \mu_2\frac{(x_0 - \mu_1)^2}{(r_2^5)_0}\right]. \end{equation} In terms of these constants we can write: \begin{equation} U_{xx} = 1 - \bar{A} + \bar{D}, \end{equation} \begin{equation} U_{yy} = 1 - \bar{A} + \bar{B}, \end{equation} \begin{equation} U_{xy} = \bar{C}. \end{equation} Of course all these \(U\)'s are real numbers and so are \(X\), \(Y\) and their derivatives. Even though these quantities are real, this does not restrict the \(\lambda_j\)'s or the \(\bar{\alpha}_j\) and \(\bar{\beta}_j\) to be real — they can be complex. The general form of the eigenvalues given by eqs(89) and (90) is: \begin{equation} \lambda_{1,2} = \pm (j_1 + i k_1), \quad \lambda_{3,4} = \pm (j_2 + i k_2), \end{equation} where \(j_1,k_1,j_2,k_2\) are real quantities and \(i=\sqrt{-1}\). We have seen that the general solution for the components of the position and velocity vectors relative to the equilibrium point involves a linear combination of terms of the form \(e^{\lambda_j t}\). This means that each \(e^{(j+ik)t}\) term will be matched by an \(e^{(j-ik)t}\) term. If the real part \(j=0\), then the paired terms reduce to sines and cosines (since \(e^{\pm i k t}=\cos(kt)\pm i\sin(kt)\)), giving an oscillatory motion. But if \(j\neq 0\) and is positive, that mode grows like \(e^{j t}\) and will blow up as time increases; in that case the perturbed solution is unstable. Therefore the equilibrium point is stable only if all the eigenvalues are purely imaginary. We will see how this works in practice by considering the general and specific cases.

Stability Of The Collinear Points

Stability of \(L4\) and \(L5\)

Following the same procedure we used for the collinear points, we can now look at the \(L_4\) Lagrange point. For this case, when \(\mu_2=0.01\), we have \[ x_0=\frac{1}{2}-\mu_2=0.49,\qquad y_0=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\approx0.8660254, \] and since \(L_4\) is one of the triangular points we have \[ (r_1)_0=(r_2)_0=1. \] Therefore: \[ U_{xx}=\frac34,\qquad U_{yy}=\frac94,\qquad U_{xy}=\frac{3\sqrt3}{4}(1-2\mu_2)\approx1.27305734356. \] So we get: \[ \mathbf{A}= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 0 & 1\\[4pt] 0.75 & 1.27305734356 & 0 & 2\\[4pt] 1.27305734356 & 2.25 & -2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \] The characteristic equation becomes: \[ \lambda^4+\lambda^2+\frac{27}{4}\mu_2(1-\mu_2)=0. \] For \(\mu_2=0.01\) this gives: \[ \lambda^4+\lambda^2+0.066825=0, \] and the eigenvalues are: \[ \lambda_1\approx+0.26834775\,i,\quad \lambda_2\approx-0.26834775\,i,\quad \lambda_3\approx+0.96332211\,i,\quad \lambda_4\approx-0.96332211\,i. \] Here all four eigenvalues are purely imaginary, so there is no exponentially growing term like we had for \(L_1,L_2\) and \(L_3\).

We use the same initial conditions as before, \(X_0=Y_0=10^{-5}\) and \(\dot{X}_0=\dot{Y}_0=0\). Using \[ \gamma_j= \frac{\lambda_j^2-U_{xx}}{2\lambda_j+U_{xy}}, \] and solving the same system for the coefficients, we get: \[ \bar\alpha_1\approx 1.72378\times10^{-5}-1.53376\times10^{-4}i, \] \[ \bar\alpha_2= \overline{\bar\alpha_1}, \] \[ \bar\alpha_3\approx -1.22378\times10^{-5}+4.27253\times10^{-5}i, \] \[ \bar\alpha_4= \overline{\bar\alpha_3}, \] and: \[ \bar\beta_1\approx 2.59997\times10^{-5}+8.80739\times10^{-5}i, \] \[ \bar\beta_2= \overline{\bar\beta_1}, \] \[ \bar\beta_3\approx -2.09997\times10^{-5}-2.45343\times10^{-5}i, \] \[ \bar\beta_4= \overline{\bar\beta_3}. \]

Alright — now that we have the necessary information we can calculate \(X(t)\) and \(Y(t)\). Since the eigenvalues come in two conjugate imaginary pairs, both parts of the solution are oscillatory. Using the two conjugate pairs we get: \begin{equation} \boxed{ \begin{aligned} X(t)\approx{}& 3.44757\times10^{-5}\cos(0.26835t) +3.06753\times10^{-4}\sin(0.26835t)\\ &-2.44757\times10^{-5}\cos(0.96332t) -8.54506\times10^{-5}\sin(0.96332t). \end{aligned}} \end{equation} And for \(Y(t)\): \begin{equation} \boxed{ \begin{aligned} Y(t)\approx{}& 5.19995\times10^{-5}\cos(0.26835t) -1.76148\times10^{-4}\sin(0.26835t)\\ &-4.19995\times10^{-5}\cos(0.96332t) +4.90686\times10^{-5}\sin(0.96332t). \end{aligned}} \end{equation}

Similerly for \(L5\) we have: \begin{equation} \boxed{ \begin{aligned} X(t)\approx{}& 4.73063190\times10^{-6}\cos(0.26834775t) +8.50624754\times10^{-5}\sin(0.26834775t)\\ &+5.26936810\times10^{-6}\cos(0.96332211t) -2.36954218\times10^{-5}\sin(0.96332211t). \end{aligned}} \end{equation} and, \begin{equation} \boxed{ \begin{aligned} Y(t)\approx{}& 2.22544268\times10^{-5}\cos(0.26834775t) +4.55426448\times10^{-5}\sin(0.26834775t)\\ &-1.22544268\times10^{-5}\cos(0.96332211t) -1.26865833\times10^{-5}\sin(0.96332211t). \end{aligned}} \end{equation}

Unlike the three collinear points, here for both \(L4\) and \(L5\) there are no terms of the form \(e^{+\lambda t}\), so for this particular case the perturbed motion remains oscillatory instead of growing exponentially.
