The Restricted Three-Body Problem

Introduction

Here we talk about the \(\textbf{The Restricted Three-Body Problem}\) (\(\textbf{R3BP}\)), The restricted three-body problem is a simplified model in celestial mechanics where two bodies (the primaries) move under their mutual gravity and a third body of negligible mass moves under their gravity but does not affect the motion of the two primaries. There are two commonly used variants of this problem: \(\textbf{ Circular R3BP}\) and \(\textbf{Elliptic R3BP}\), In The Circular R3BP we study the special case where the two primaries move on circular orbits about their barycenter and in Elliptic R3BP primaries move on elliptical orbits which is more realistic though much harder.

Here we'll derive the equations of motion for the circular restricted three-body problem, and explore different derivations, Lagrange points, zero-velocity curves / Hill regions, chaos and resonances, etc. Of course, in real life orbits aren't circular, but the circular restricted three-body problem works as a good approximation to explore different properties of the solar system. Studying the R3BP is both practically useful (spacecraft trajectories, asteroid dynamics) and great for building intuition about phase-space structures, transport, and stability. Overall, it gives you a compact place to learn the geometric and dynamical ideas that govern many real systems in astronomy and astrodynamics.

Throughout history many people have contributed to this problem. The giants of mathematics who tackled it and made important contributions include Euler, Lagrange, Laplace, Jacobi, Le Verrier, Hamilton, Poincaré, and Birkhoff. A lot has been done since then, Research continues today, and the R3BP remains a fruitful area for both theoretical work and practical applications.

The Equation Of Motion

In the image, \(O\) is the barycenter of \(m_1\) and \(m_2\), and \(n\) is the angular frequency of the two masses (We are assume this for now later we will show proof that n is actually angular frequency of the two masses, rememer we choose such unites so that \(G=1\)) \begin{equation} n=\sqrt{\frac{G\left(m_1+m_2\right)}{a^3}}, \end{equation} Here \((\xi,\eta,\zeta)\) are the barycentric coordinates and \((x,y,z)\) are the coordinates in the rotating frame that rotates with the two bodies (here \(\zeta\) and \(z\) are perpendicular to the screen). Using Newton's law of gravitation, we can derive the equations of motion and we get: \begin{equation} \ddot{\xi}=\mu_1\frac{\xi_1 - \xi}{{r'_{1}}^{3}} + \mu_2\frac{\xi_2 - \xi}{{r'_{2}}^{3}} \end{equation} \begin{equation} \ddot{\eta}=\mu_1\frac{\eta_1 - \eta}{{r'_{1}}^{3}} + \mu_2\frac{\eta_2 - \eta}{{r'_{2}}^{3}} \end{equation} \begin{equation} \ddot{\zeta}=\mu_1\frac{\zeta_1 - \zeta}{{r'_{1}}^{3}} + \mu_2\frac{\zeta_2 - \zeta}{{r'_{2}}^{3}}. \end{equation} Here, \(r_1'\) and \(r_2'\) are: \begin{equation} {r_1'}^2 = (\xi_1 - \xi)^2 + (\eta_1 - \eta)^2 + (\zeta_1 - \zeta)^2 \end{equation} \begin{equation} {r_2'}^2 = (\xi_2 - \xi)^2 + (\eta_2 - \eta)^2 + (\zeta_2 - \zeta)^2 \end{equation} In the inertial frame barycentric coordinate at time \(0\) let two masses are at \(\xi_1= -a\bar{\mu}\) and \(\xi_2=a(1- \bar{\mu})\) where \(\bar{\mu}=\frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2}\) (assumeing \(m_1>m_2\)), meaning coordinates of the two bodies (at time \(t=0\)) are \((\xi_1,\eta_1,\zeta_1)=(\xi_1,0,0)=(-\frac{am_2}{m_1+m_2},0,0)\) and \((\xi_2,\eta_2,\zeta_2)=(\xi_2,0,0)=(\frac{am_1}{m_1+m_2},0,0)\). So at any moment of time the coordinates of the two masses are in rotating frame is \((x_1,y_1,z_1)=(x_1,0,0)=(-\frac{am_2}{m_1+m_2},0,0)\) and \((x_2,y_2,z_2)=(x_2,0,0)=(\frac{am_1}{m_1+m_2},0,0)\).From now let's use \(\mu_1=1- \bar{\mu}\) and \(\mu_2=\bar{\mu}\), so \((x_1,y_1,z_1)=(-a\mu_2,0,0)\) and \((x_2,y_2,z_2)=(a\mu_1,0,0)\). So in the rotating frame (we from now on will use \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) to denote the position vectors from \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) to the particle). \begin{equation} {r_1}^2=(x+a\mu_2)^2+y^2+z^2 \end{equation} \begin{equation} {r_2}^2=(x-a\mu_1)^2+y^2+z^2 \end{equation} Here, \((x,y,z)\) are the position of the particle. We can relate the barycentric coordinates with the rotating frame by the following equation — if you are somewhat familiar with linear algebra you will know what I am talking about: \begin{equation} \begin{pmatrix}\xi \\ \eta \\ \zeta\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(nt) & -\sin(nt) & 0 \\ \sin(nt) & \cos(nt) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}x \\ y \\ z\end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{R}(t) \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{\Xi} \end{equation} If we now differentiate it we get: \begin{equation} \dot{\mathbf{\Xi}}=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\mathbf{R}(t)\mathbf{X}\right)=\dot{\mathbf{R}(t)}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{R}(t)\dot{\mathbf{X}} \end{equation} Here, \[ \dot{\mathbf{R}(t)} = n\begin{pmatrix} -\sin(nt) & -\cos(nt) & 0 \\ \cos(nt) & -\sin(nt) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \] We can write it as, \[ \dot{\mathbf{R}(t)} = \mathbf{R}(t) \mathbf{J} \] Where, \[ \mathbf{J} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -n & 0 \\ n & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \] So we can rewrite, \[ \dot{\mathbf{\Xi}}=\dot{\mathbf{R}}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{R}\dot{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{R}\left(\mathbf{JX}+\mathbf{\dot{X}}\right) \] and, \[ \mathbf{JX}=\begin{pmatrix}-ny \\ nx \\ 0\end{pmatrix} \] Putting all this together we get, \begin{equation}\boxed{ \begin{pmatrix}\dot{\xi} \\ \dot{\eta} \\ \dot{\zeta}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(nt) & -\sin(nt) & 0 \\ \sin(nt) & \cos(nt) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\dot{x}-ny \\ \dot{y}+nx \\ \dot{z}\end{pmatrix}} \end{equation} Doing the same thing again we get: \begin{equation} \boxed{ \begin{pmatrix}\ddot{\xi} \\ \ddot{\eta} \\ \ddot{\zeta}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(nt) & -\sin(nt) & 0 \\ \sin(nt) & \cos(nt) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\ddot{x}-2n\dot{y}-n^2 x \\ \ddot{y}+2n\dot{x} + n^2 y\\ \ddot{z}\end{pmatrix}} \end{equation} If you look carefully you will see that the rotating reference frame has introduced terms like \(n\dot{y}\), \(n\dot{x}\); these are Coriolis accelerations, and \(n^2 x\) and \(n^2 y\) are the centrifugal accelerations in the equation of motion. We can substitute in for \(\xi,\eta,\zeta,\ddot{\xi},\ddot{\eta}\) and \(\ddot{\zeta}\) in Eq(27), (28) and (29); then we get: \begin{equation} (\ddot{x} - 2n\dot{y} - n^2 x)\cos nt - (\ddot{y} + 2n\dot{x} - n^2 y)\sin nt = \left[ \mu_1 \frac{x_1 - x}{{r_1}^3} + \mu_2 \frac{x_2 - x}{{r_2}^3} \right]\cos nt + \left[ \frac{\mu_1}{{r_1}^3} + \frac{\mu_2}{{r_2}^3} \right] y \sin nt \end{equation} \begin{equation} (\ddot{x} - 2n\dot{y} - n^2 x)\sin nt + (\ddot{y} + 2n\dot{x} - n^2 y)\cos nt = \left[ \mu_1 \frac{x_1 - x}{{r_1}^3} + \mu_2 \frac{x_2 - x}{{r_2}^3} \right]\sin nt - \left[ \frac{\mu_1}{{r_1}^3} + \frac{\mu_2}{{r_2}^3} \right] y \cos nt \end{equation} \begin{equation} \ddot{z} = - \left[ \frac{\mu_1}{{r_1}^3} + \frac{\mu_2}{{r_2}^3} \right] z. \end{equation} If we multiply Eq(38) by cos nt, and Eq(39) by sin nt, and add the results; and then multiply Eq(38) by sin nt, and Eq(40) by cos nt, and add these together, the equations of motion in the synodic system become \begin{align} \ddot{x} - 2n\dot{y} - n^2 x &= - \left[ \mu_1 \frac{x + a\mu_2}{{r_1}^3} + \mu_2 \frac{x - a\mu_1}{{r_2}^3} \right], \\[1em] \ddot{y} + 2n\dot{x} - n^2 y &= - \left[ \frac{\mu_1}{{r_1}^3} + \frac{\mu_2}{{r_2}^3} \right] y, \\[1em] \ddot{z} &= - \left[ \frac{\mu_1}{{r_1}^3} + \frac{\mu_2}{{r_2}^3} \right] z. \end{align}

These accelerations can also be written as the gradient of a scalar function \(U\): \begin{equation} \ddot{x}-2n\dot{y}=\frac{\partial U}{\partial x} \end{equation} \begin{equation} \ddot{y}+2n\dot{x}=\frac{\partial U}{\partial y} \end{equation} \begin{equation} \ddot{z}=\frac{\partial U}{\partial z} \end{equation} Here, \begin{equation} U(x,y,z)=\left[\frac{n^2}{2}\left(x^2+y^2\right)+\frac{\mu_1}{r_1}+\frac{\mu_2}{r_2}\right] \end{equation} So, we can write the equaton of moion as, \begin{equation} \mathbf{\ddot{r}}+2\mathbf{\Omega} \times \mathbf{\dot{r}} = \nabla U \end{equation} I want to clarify something. Here the effective potential is not in the usual convention because we removed the negative sign. This does not change the equations of motion since we also removed the minus on the right-hand side of those equations. If you want, you can of course use the conventional form and write \[ \ddot{x}-2n\dot{y}=-\frac{\partial U^*}{\partial x},\qquad \ddot{y}+2n\dot{x}=-\frac{\partial U^*}{\partial y},\qquad \ddot{z}=-\frac{\partial U^*}{\partial z}, \] where \(U^*=-U\). In vector form, \[ \ddot{\mathbf r}+2\Omega\times\dot{\mathbf r}=-\nabla U^*. \]

The Jacobi Integral

The Jacobi integral (or Jacobi constant) is a conserved quantity in the circular restricted three-body problem (in the rotating frame); the numerical value of the Jacobi constant — which depends on the particle’s position and velocity for that orbit — determines the allowed and forbidden regions of motion. Here we are going to talk about the Jacobi constant and its derivation and implications.

Derivation

We have the equation (44), (45) and (46), if we multiply eq(44) by \(\dot{x}\) and multiply eq(45) by \(\dot{y}\) and multiply eq(46) by \(\dot{z}\) and then add all them up then we get: \[ \ddot{x}\dot{x}-2n\dot{y}\dot{x} + \ddot{y}\dot{y} + 2n\dot{y}\dot{x} + \ddot{z}\dot{z} = \frac{\partial U}{\partial x} \frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial y} \frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial z} \frac{dz}{dt} \] So we get, \begin{equation} \ddot{x}\dot{x} + \ddot{y}\dot{y} + \ddot{z}\dot{z} = \frac{\partial U}{\partial x} \frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial y} \frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial z} \frac{dz}{dt} \end{equation} We can recongnize the fact that \(\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\dot{x}^2 \right)= \ddot{x}\dot{x}\), So left side of the equation can be writen as: \[ \frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2 + \dot{z}^2 \right)\right] = \frac{1}{2} \frac{d}{dt}\left(\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2 + \dot{z}^2 \right) = \frac{1}{2} \frac{d}{dt}\left(v^2 \right) \] Where, \(v^2=\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2 + \dot{z}^2\). So, equation(49) can be writen as, \[ \frac{1}{2} \frac{d}{dt}\left(v^2\right) = \frac{dU}{dt} \] \(\left(\text{Note: } \frac{dU}{dt} = \frac{\partial U}{\partial x} \frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial y} \frac{dy}{dt} + \frac{\partial U}{\partial z} \frac{dz}{dt} \right)\) Now, if we intigrate both side then we get: \[\int \frac{1}{2} \frac{d}{dt}(v^2)\, dt \;=\; \int \frac{dU}{dt}\, dt \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \frac{1}{2} v^2 \;=\; U + C_1\] \[\Rightarrow v^2=2U+2C_1\] Let, \(2C_1=-C_J\) then we get: \begin{equation} v^2=2U-C_J \end{equation} Alright, now we already know what \(U\) is: it is just the effective potential energy in equation (47). Substituting into that equation we get \[ \dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2 + \dot{z}^2 = \bigg[n^2\big(x^2+y^2\big) + 2\Big(\frac{\mu_1}{r_1}+\frac{\mu_2}{r_2}\Big)\bigg] - C_J \] and therefore \begin{equation} C_J = \bigg[n^2\big(x^2+y^2\big) + 2\Big(\frac{\mu_1}{r_1}+\frac{\mu_2}{r_2}\Big)\bigg] - \dot{x}^2 - \dot{y}^2 - \dot{z}^2 . \end{equation} This is what we call the Jacobi constant \(C_J\); it is a conserved quantity for a given orbit. Here, as you can see, it is written in terms of the rotating-frame coordinates. We can indeed transform it to sidereal (inertial) coordinates and hope that we might get some terms that give deeper intuition about the Jacobi constant. We have, \[ \begin{pmatrix}x \\ y \\ z\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(nt) & \sin(nt) & 0 \\ -\sin(nt) & \cos(nt) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\xi \\ \eta \\ \zeta\end{pmatrix} \] Using eq. (36), \[ \begin{pmatrix}\dot{x}-ny \\ \dot{y}+nx \\ \dot{z}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(nt) & \sin(nt) & 0 \\ -\sin(nt) & \cos(nt) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\dot{\xi} \\ \dot{\eta} \\ \dot{\zeta}\end{pmatrix} \] \[ \Rightarrow \begin{pmatrix} \dot x - n y\\[4pt] \dot y + n x\\[4pt] \dot z \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \dot x\\[4pt] \dot y\\[4pt] \dot z \end{pmatrix} + n \begin{pmatrix} \sin(nt) & -\cos(nt) & 0\\[4pt] \cos(nt) & \sin(nt) & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \xi\\[4pt] \eta\\[4pt] \zeta \end{pmatrix} \] \[ \Rightarrow \begin{pmatrix} \dot x\\[4pt] \dot y\\[4pt] \dot z \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(nt) & \sin(nt) & 0\\[4pt] -\sin(nt) & \cos(nt) & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \dot{\xi}\\[4pt] \dot{\eta}\\[4pt] \dot{\zeta} \end{pmatrix} - n \Pi_1 \begin{pmatrix} \xi\\[4pt] \eta\\[4pt] \zeta \end{pmatrix}. \] We want to compute \(\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2 +\dot{z}^2 = \begin{pmatrix}\dot x & \dot y & \dot z\end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix}\dot x\\[4pt]\dot y\\[4pt]\dot z\end{pmatrix}\), so: \[ \dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2 +\dot{z}^2 = \left( \Pi_0 \Xi - n \Pi_1 \dot{\Xi}\right)^T \left( \Pi_0 \Xi - n \Pi_1 \dot{\Xi}\right) = \dot{\Xi}^T \Pi_0^T \Pi_0 \dot{\Xi} - n \dot{\Xi}^T \Pi_0^T \Pi_1 \Xi - n \Xi^T \Pi_1^T \Pi_0 \dot{\Xi} + n^2 \Xi^T \Pi_1^T \Pi_1 \Xi \end{equation} Here, \(\Pi_0\) is a rotation matrix and we know rotation matrices are orthogonal. So, using our knowledge of linear algebra we can say \(\Pi_0^T \Pi_0 = \mathbb{I}_3\) (here, \(\mathbb{I}_3\) is the \(3 \times 3\) identity matrix), and hence \(\dot{\Xi}^T \Pi_0^T \Pi_0 \dot{\Xi} = \dot{\xi}^2 + \dot{\eta}^2 + \dot{\zeta}^2\). We can compute \(\Pi_1^T \Pi_1\); then we get: \begin{equation} \Pi_1^T \Pi_1 = \begin{pmatrix} \sin^2 nt + \cos^2 nt & 0 & 0 \\[6pt] 0 & \sin^2 nt + \cos^2 nt & 0 \\[6pt] 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\[6pt] 0 & 1 & 0 \\[6pt] 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation} And we get \(\Xi^T \Pi_1^2 \Pi_1 \Xi = \xi^2 + \eta^2\). Then we have the terms \(- n\,\dot{\xi}^{T} \Pi_0^{T} \Pi_1 \xi\) and \(- n\,\xi^{T} \Pi_1^{T} \Pi_0 \dot{\xi}\). These two scalars are transposes of each other, so their sum is \(-2n\,\dot{\xi}^{T} \Pi_0^{T} \Pi_1 \xi\). Here \(\Pi_0^{T} \Pi_1\) is: \[ \Pi_0^{T} \Pi_1 = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(nt) & -\sin(nt) & 0\\[4pt] \sin(nt) & \cos(nt) & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \sin(nt) & -\cos(nt) & 0\\[4pt] \cos(nt) & \sin(nt) & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0\\[4pt] 1 & 0 & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \] Thus, \[ -2n\,\dot{\xi}^{T} \Pi_0^{T} \Pi_1 \xi = -2n\dot{\xi}^{T} A^{T} B \xi = -2n\big(\dot\xi(-\eta) + \dot\eta(\xi) + \dot\zeta\cdot 0\big) = -2n\big(-\xi\dot\eta + \eta\dot\xi\big) = -2n\big(\eta\dot\xi - \xi\dot\eta\big) = 2n\big(\xi\dot\eta - \eta\dot\xi\big). \] Now substituting our results into equation (52) we get: \begin{equation} \dot x^{2} + \dot y^{2} + \dot z^{2} = \dot\xi^{2} + \dot\eta^{2} + \dot\zeta^{2} + n^{2}\big(\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}\big) + 2n\big(\xi\dot\eta - \eta\dot\xi\big). \end{equation} We have, \[ \begin{pmatrix}x \\ y \\ z\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(nt) & \sin(nt) & 0 \\ -\sin(nt) & \cos(nt) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\xi \\ \eta \\ \zeta\end{pmatrix} \] Using eq. (36), \[ \begin{pmatrix}\dot{x}-ny \\ \dot{y}+nx \\ \dot{z}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(nt) & \sin(nt) & 0 \\ -\sin(nt) & \cos(nt) & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\dot{\xi} \\ \dot{\eta} \\ \dot{\zeta}\end{pmatrix} \] \[ \Rightarrow \begin{pmatrix} \dot x - n y\\[4pt] \dot y + n x\\[4pt] \dot z \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \dot x\\[4pt] \dot y\\[4pt] \dot z \end{pmatrix} + n \begin{pmatrix} \sin(nt) & -\cos(nt) & 0\\[4pt] \cos(nt) & \sin(nt) & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \xi\\[4pt] \eta\\[4pt] \zeta \end{pmatrix} \] \[ \Rightarrow \begin{pmatrix} \dot x\\[4pt] \dot y\\[4pt] \dot z \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(nt) & \sin(nt) & 0\\[4pt] -\sin(nt) & \cos(nt) & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \dot{\xi}\\[4pt] \dot{\eta}\\[4pt] \dot{\zeta} \end{pmatrix} - n \begin{pmatrix} \sin(nt) & -\cos(nt) & 0\\[4pt] \cos(nt) & \sin(nt) & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \xi\\[4pt] \eta\\[4pt] \zeta \end{pmatrix}. \] Let, \begin{equation} \Pi_0 = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(nt) & \sin(nt) & 0\\[4pt] -\sin(nt) & \cos(nt) & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \quad , \quad \Pi_1 = \begin{pmatrix} \sin(nt) & -\cos(nt) & 0\\[4pt] \cos(nt) & \sin(nt) & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \end{equation} So, \[ \begin{pmatrix} \dot x\\[4pt] \dot y\\[4pt] \dot z \end{pmatrix} = \Pi_0 \begin{pmatrix} \dot{\xi}\\[4pt] \dot{\eta}\\[4pt] \dot{\zeta} \end{pmatrix} - n \Pi_1 \begin{pmatrix} \xi\\[4pt] \eta\\[4pt] \zeta \end{pmatrix}. \] We want to compute \(\dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2 +\dot{z}^2 = \begin{pmatrix}\dot x & \dot y & \dot z\end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix}\dot x\\[4pt]\dot y\\[4pt]\dot z\end{pmatrix}\), so: \[ \dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2 +\dot{z}^2 = \left( \Pi_0 \Xi - n \Pi_1 \dot{\Xi}\right)^T \left( \Pi_0 \Xi - n \Pi_1 \dot{\Xi}\right) \] Here, \(\Xi=\begin{pmatrix}\xi\\[4pt]\eta\\[4pt]\zeta\end{pmatrix}\). \begin{equation} \dot{x}^2 + \dot{y}^2 +\dot{z}^2 = \left( \Pi_0 \Xi - n \Pi_1 \dot{\Xi}\right)^T \left( \Pi_0 \Xi - n \Pi_1 \dot{\Xi}\right) = \dot{\Xi}^T \Pi_0^T \Pi_0 \dot{\Xi} - n \dot{\Xi}^T \Pi_0^T \Pi_1 \Xi - n \Xi^T \Pi_1^T \Pi_0 \dot{\Xi} + n^2 \Xi^T \Pi_1^T \Pi_1 \Xi \end{equation} Here, \(\Pi_0\) is a rotation matrix and we know rotation matrices are orthogonal. So, using our knowledge of linear algebra we can say \(\Pi_0^T \Pi_0 = \mathbb{I}_3\) (here, \(\mathbb{I}_3\) is the \(3 \times 3\) identity matrix), and hence \(\dot{\Xi}^T \Pi_0^T \Pi_0 \dot{\Xi} = \dot{\xi}^2 + \dot{\eta}^2 + \dot{\zeta}^2\). We can compute \(\Pi_1^T \Pi_1\); then we get: \begin{equation} \Pi_1^T \Pi_1 = \begin{pmatrix} \sin^2 nt + \cos^2 nt & 0 & 0 \\[6pt] 0 & \sin^2 nt + \cos^2 nt & 0 \\[6pt] 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\[6pt] 0 & 1 & 0 \\[6pt] 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{equation} And we get \(\Xi^T \Pi_1^2 \Pi_1 \Xi = \xi^2 + \eta^2\). Then we have the terms \(- n\,\dot{\xi}^{T} \Pi_0^{T} \Pi_1 \xi\) and \(- n\,\xi^{T} \Pi_1^{T} \Pi_0 \dot{\xi}\). These two scalars are transposes of each other, so their sum is \(-2n\,\dot{\xi}^{T} \Pi_0^{T} \Pi_1 \xi\). Here \(\Pi_0^{T} \Pi_1\) is: \[ \Pi_0^{T} \Pi_1 = \begin{pmatrix} \cos(nt) & -\sin(nt) & 0\\[4pt] \sin(nt) & \cos(nt) & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \sin(nt) & -\cos(nt) & 0\\[4pt] \cos(nt) & \sin(nt) & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0\\[4pt] 1 & 0 & 0\\[4pt] 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \] Thus, \[ -2n\,\dot{\xi}^{T} \Pi_0^{T} \Pi_1 \xi = -2n\dot{\xi}^{T} A^{T} B \xi = -2n\big(\dot\xi(-\eta) + \dot\eta(\xi) + \dot\zeta\cdot 0\big) = -2n\big(-\xi\dot\eta + \eta\dot\xi\big) = -2n\big(\eta\dot\xi - \xi\dot\eta\big) = 2n\big(\xi\dot\eta - \eta\dot\xi\big). \] Now substituting our results into equation (52) we get: \begin{equation} \dot x^{2} + \dot y^{2} + \dot z^{2} = \dot\xi^{2} + \dot\eta^{2} + \dot\zeta^{2} + n^{2}\big(\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}\big) + 2n\big(\xi\dot\eta - \eta\dot\xi\big). \end{equation} From Eq. (51) we have: \[ C_J = \bigg[n^2\big(x^2+y^2\big) + 2\Big(\frac{\mu_1}{r_1}+\frac{\mu_2}{r_2}\Big)\bigg] - \dot{x}^2 - \dot{y}^2 - \dot{z}^2 \] One thing I want to let you know, in case you didn't know, is that since we are using a rotation matrix to linearly transform from the inertial frame to the rotating frame, lengths are invariant. Meaning: if a vector \(\mathbf{a}\) in the inertial frame has length \(|\mathbf{a}|\), then the same vector in the rotating frame also has length \(|\mathbf{a}|\). So we can say \(x^2+y^2+z^2=\xi^2+\eta^2+\zeta^2\). Since our primaries and the particle are in the ((x,y)) plane, substituting Eq. (59) into \(C_J\) we can rewrite \(C_J\) as: \[ C_J = \bigg[n^2\big(\xi^2+\eta^2\big) + 2\Big(\frac{\mu_1}{r_1}+\frac{\mu_2}{r_2}\Big)\bigg] - \left(\dot\xi^{2} + \dot\eta^{2} + \dot\zeta^{2} + n^{2}\big(\xi^{2}+\eta^{2}\big) + 2n\big(\xi\dot\eta - \eta\dot\xi\big) \right) \] and it simplifies to: \begin{equation} C_J = 2\Big(\frac{\mu_1}{r_1}+\frac{\mu_2}{r_2}\Big) + 2n\big(\xi\dot\eta -\eta\dot\xi \big) - \dot\xi^{2} - \dot\eta^{2} - \dot\zeta^{2} \end{equation} Here, the angular velocity vector is along the \(z\)-axis, so \(\mathbf{n}=\left(0,0,n\right)\). Let’s define \(\mathbf{h}\) as the specific angular momentum vector (angular momentum per unit mass). \(\mathbf{h}\) is: \[ \mathbf{h} = \Xi \times \dot{\Xi} = \left(\xi \dot{\eta} - \eta \dot{\xi}\right)\hat{\zeta} = \left(\xi \dot{\eta} - \eta \dot{\xi}\right)\hat{z} \] (Here, \(\hat{\zeta}\) and \(\hat{z}\) are unit vectors along the \(z\)-axis.) So we can write Eq. (60) as \begin{equation} \frac{1}{2} \left(\dot\xi^{2} + \dot\eta^{2} + \dot\zeta^{2}\right) - \Big(\frac{\mu_1}{r_1}+\frac{\mu_2}{r_2}\Big) =\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{n} - \frac{1}{2} C_J \end{equation} If you have a question about the physical significance of \(\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}\), you can think of this term as measuring the coupling between the particle’s spin about the rotation axis and the rotation of the frame (or the primaries). Alright — here, as you can see, the left-hand side of the equation is the total, or mechanical, energy per unit mass of the particle, and since \(\mathbf{h}\cdot\mathbf{n}\) is not constant, the mechanical energy of the test particle is not conserved in the rotating frame. Alright — now that we have finished deriving the Jacobi constant, let’s dive back into what we were talking about before the derivation. We can use the Jacobi constant to determine regions from which the particle is excluded. One simple way to see what are uses of the Jacobi constant is to check where the particle comes to a stop. At those locations, we find that \begin{equation} 2U = C_J = n^2(x^2+y^2) + 2\left(\frac{\mu_1}{r_1} + \frac{\mu_2}{r_2}\right) \end{equation} Because \(C_J\) is constant, Eq. (62) describes a set of points or surfaces where the particle's velocity is zero. These surfaces are called \(\mathbf{zero\text{-}velocity\ surfaces}\).

That means if the particle is inside one of those enclosed regions it cannot leave for that Jacobi constant and will continue to orbit that primary unless an active maneuver changes its Jacobi constant. And that is what we see in the second video: the particle is in the region of the right primary, and the right primary is enclosed by the zero-velocity surface, so the particle continues to orbit the primary and will not leave the enclosed region — because to do so its Jacobi constant would first have to change.
