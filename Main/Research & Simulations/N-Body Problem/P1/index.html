Abstract

Here we study the N-body problem in a gravitational field, gradually going from the two-body problem to the three-body problem and beyond, and then simulate the solar system. Along the way, we will analyze each problem to gain a deeper understanding of the subject, and finally explore how we can find stable periodic orbits for any N-body problem, given sufficient computational power.

The purpose of this work is to give the reader a deeper understanding of the topic through simulations and to verify our established understanding of the subject.

2-Body Problem

The 2-body central-force problem is an essentially very important topic to study because it gives us a good idea and better intuition about the N-body problem and will help us as we go along.

In a central force the potential function is: \begin{equation} U(\mathbf{r_1},\mathbf{r_2})= \frac{-\gamma}{|\mathbf{r_1}-\mathbf{r_2}|}=U(|\mathbf{r_1}-\mathbf{r_2}|) \end{equation} For gravitational force \(\gamma\) is just \(G m_1 m_2\). We could also apply it to electric charges, then \(\gamma\) is \(\frac{q_1 q_2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\), and here, \begin{equation} r = |\mathbf{r_1}-\mathbf{r_2}| \end{equation}

And so the gravitational potential function is, \begin{equation} U(r)=-\frac{G m_1 m_2}{r} \end{equation}

Therefore the Lagrangian for this system is: \begin{equation} \mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2} m_1 \dot{\mathbf{r_1}}^2 + \frac{1}{2} m_2 \dot{\mathbf{r_2}}^2 - U(r) \end{equation}

We can write this Lagrangian in terms of the center-of-mass vector \(\mathbf{R}\) and the relative position vector \(\mathbf{r}\). The center of mass is: \begin{equation} \mathbf{R}=\frac{m_1 \mathbf{r_1}+m_2 \mathbf{r_2}}{m_1 + m_2}=\frac{m_1 \mathbf{r_1}+m_2 \mathbf{r_2}}{M} \end{equation} and therefore, \begin{equation} \mathbf{r}_1 = \mathbf{R} + \frac{m_2}{M}\mathbf{r} \quad\text{and}\quad \mathbf{r}_2 = \mathbf{R} - \frac{m_1}{M}\mathbf{r} \end{equation} Then the kinetic energy is: \[ T=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(m_1 \bigl[\dot{\mathbf{R}}+\frac{m_2}{M} \dot{\mathbf{r}}\bigr]^2 + m_2 \bigl[\dot{\mathbf{R}}-\frac{m_1}{M} \dot{\mathbf{r}}\bigr]^2 \bigr) \] \begin{equation} T=\frac{1}{2}M\dot{\mathbf{R}}^2+\frac{1}{2}\mu \dot{\mathbf{r}}^2 \quad\text{where}\quad \mu=\frac{m_1 m_2}{M} \end{equation} So, the Lagrangian becomes: \begin{equation} \mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}M\dot{\mathbf{R}}^2+\frac{1}{2}\mu \dot{\mathbf{r}}^2 - U(r) \end{equation} We can choose the center of mass as our reference frame, where \(\dot{\mathbf{R}}=0\). Since \(\mathbf{R}\) is an ignorable coordinate: \begin{equation} \mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{2}\mu \dot{\mathbf{r}}^2 - U(r) \end{equation}

Now we can use the Euler-Lagrange equation to obtain the equation of motion, \begin{equation} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial q}-\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{q}}\bigr)=0 \end{equation} But before that we have to find what \(U(r)\) is. One might think there is only the gravitational force, so \(U(r)\) is just the gravitational potential: \begin{equation} U(r)=-\frac{G m_1 m_2}{r} \end{equation} However, there is another effect responsible for planetary orbits: the centrifugal force. We can use polar coordinates for our Lagrangian and then we get: \begin{equation} \mathcal{L}= \frac{1}{2}\mu \bigl(\dot{r}^2 + r^2 \dot{\phi}^2\bigr) - U(r) \end{equation} Since \(\phi\) is an ignorable coordinate, applying the Euler-Lagrange equation gives: \begin{equation} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{\phi}}= \mu r^2 \dot{\phi} \end{equation} which is a constant; let this constant be \(l\). Thus: \begin{equation} \mu r^2 \dot{\phi}=l \end{equation} Applying the Euler-Lagrange equation for the radial coordinate yields: \begin{equation} \mu r \dot{\phi}^2 - \frac{dU}{dr}=\mu \ddot{r} \end{equation} Here \(-\frac{dU}{dr}\) is the gravitational force and the \(\mu r \dot{\phi}^2\) term is the centrifugal force. We can associate the centrifugal force with a potential function where: \[\frac{dU_{cf}}{dr}=\mu r \dot{\phi}^2\] and so \(U_{cf}\) is: \begin{equation} U_{cf}(r)=\frac{l^2}{2\mu r^2} \end{equation} Therefore we can introduce a new effective potential: \begin{equation} U_{eff}(r) = U(r) + \frac{l^2}{2\mu r^2} = U(r) + U_{cf}(r) \end{equation} So we can rewrite the r-equation as: \begin{equation} \mu \ddot{r} = -\frac{dU_{eff}}{dr} \end{equation}

Here \(l\) is the total angular momentum of the system, which is constant and can be calculated from the initial conditions. The total angular momentum of the system is: \begin{equation} \mathbf{L}=m_1\mathbf{r_1} \times \dot{\mathbf{r_1}} + m_2\mathbf{r_2} \times \dot{\mathbf{r_2}} \end{equation} Since \begin{equation} \mathbf{r_1}=\frac{m_2}{M}\mathbf{r}, \quad \mathbf{r_2}=-\frac{m_1}{M}\mathbf{r} \end{equation} we can substitute into the above expression to obtain: \begin{equation} \mathbf{L}= \mathbf{r} \times \mu\dot{\mathbf{r}} \end{equation}

In the image we have two masses and their positions from the CM: \(\mathbf{r_1}\) and \(\mathbf{r_2}\). The relative position vector is: \[ \mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}x_1 \\ y_1\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}x_2 \\ y_2\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}x_1-x_2 \\ y_1-y_2\end{pmatrix} \]

The relative velocity is: \[ \dot{\mathbf{r}}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{x_1} \\ v_{y_1}\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}v_{x_2} \\ v_{y_2}\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}v_{x_1}-v_{x_2} \\ v_{y_1}-v_{y_2}\end{pmatrix} \]

Now we can calculate the total angular momentum for the initial conditions by the cross product: \[ \mathbf{r}\times\dot{\mathbf{r}} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{e}_x & \mathbf{e}_y & \mathbf{e}_z \\ x_1 - x_2 & y_1 - y_2 & 0 \\ v_{x_1}-v_{x_2} & v_{y_1}-v_{y_2} & 0 \end{vmatrix} = \bigl[(x_1 - x_2)(v_{y_1}-v_{y_2}) - (y_1 - y_2)(v_{x_1}-v_{x_2})\bigr]\,\mathbf{e}_z. \]

Remember that the plane in which we calculate \(\mathbf{r}\times\dot{\mathbf{r}}\) is the cross-sectional plane of the orbits, and \(\mathbf{L}\) is perpendicular to that plane.

When we are given the initial conditions \(x_{10}\), \(y_{10}\), \(x_{20}\), \(y_{20}\), \(v_{x_{10}}\), \(v_{y_{10}}\), \(v_{x_{20}}\), and \(v_{y_{20}}\), we can easily calculate the magnitude of the total angular momentum \(l\). So: \begin{equation} \mathbf{L}_0= \mu \bigl[(x_{10} - x_{20})(v_{y_{10}}-v_{y_{20}}) - (y_{10} - y_{20})(v_{x_{10}}-v_{x_{20}})\bigr]\,\mathbf{e}_z\end{equation} and \(l\) is: \begin{equation} l= \mu \bigl[(x_{10} - x_{20})(v_{y_{10}}-v_{y_{20}}) - (y_{10} - y_{20})(v_{x_{10}}-v_{x_{20}})\bigr]\end{equation}

Equation Of Orbit: Solving the Radial Equation as a Function of \(\phi\)

We have the radial equation and the \(\phi\) equation; now our goal is to get the solution \(r(\phi)\). First, I will numerically solve it, and later we will simulate the two-body problem to justify our solution.

Radial equation: \(\mu\,\ddot{r} = -\frac{dU}{dr} + \frac{l^2}{\mu\,r^3}\) \(\phi\) equation: \(\dot{\phi} = \frac{l}{\mu\,r^2}\) Here, \(F(r) = -\frac{dU}{dr}\); for gravity, \(F(r) = -\frac{\gamma}{r^2}\). To rewrite the radial equation in terms of \(\phi\), substitute \[ u = \frac{1}{r}, \quad r = \frac{1}{u}, \] and use the chain rule: \[ \frac{d}{dt} = \frac{d\phi}{dt}\,\frac{d}{d\phi} = \dot{\phi}\,\frac{d}{d\phi} = \frac{l}{\mu\,r^2}\,\frac{d}{d\phi} = \frac{l\,u^2}{\mu}\,\frac{d}{d\phi}. \] Substituting into the radial equation gives: \(-\frac{l^2\,u^2}{\mu}\,\frac{d^2u}{d\phi^2} = F + \frac{l^2\,u^3}{\mu}\). Hence, \(\displaystyle u''(\phi) = -u(\phi) - \frac{\mu}{l^2\,u(\phi)^2}\,F. \) Since \(F = -\gamma/r^2 = \gamma\,u^2\), we get: \(\displaystyle u''(\phi) = -u(\phi) + \frac{\mu\,\gamma}{l^2}. \) Notice for an inverse-square force the last term is constant, which simplifies the solution. Set \[ w(\phi) = -u(\phi) + \frac{\mu\,\gamma}{l^2}, \] giving \[ w''(\phi) = -w(\phi). \] Its general solution is: \(w(\phi) = A \cos(\phi - \delta)\). By choosing \(\phi=0\) along the periapsis, we take \(\delta=0\). Thus, \[ u(\phi) = \frac{\mu\gamma}{l^2} + A\cos(\phi) = \frac{\mu\gamma}{l^2}\bigl(1 + \epsilon\cos\phi\bigr), \] and therefore \(\displaystyle r(\phi) = \frac{c}{1 + \epsilon\cos\phi}, \quad c = \frac{l^2}{\mu\gamma} = \frac{l^2}{G\,m_1\,m_2\,\mu}. \) So we have equation of the orbit is: \begin{equation} \boxed{ r(\phi) = \frac{c}{1 + \epsilon\cos\phi}} \end{equation}

Notice this is the equation for an ellipce.

Conservation of Energy

We have the eq.18 and we can multiply both sides of that equation by \(\dot{r}\) and we obtain: \[ \mu\ddot{r}\frac{dr}{dt}=-\frac{dr}{dt}\frac{dU_{eff}}{dr} \] \[ \Rightarrow \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mu\dot{r}^2\right) =-\frac{d}{dt}\left(U_{eff}\right) \] \[ \Rightarrow \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mu\dot{r}^2 + U_{eff}\right) =0 \] That means: \[\frac{1}{2}\mu\dot{r}^2 + U_{eff}=constent\] Therefore total energy of the system is: \[ \frac{1}{2}\mu\dot{r}^2 + U_{eff}=\frac{1}{2}\mu\dot{r}^2 + U(r) + \frac{l^2}{2\mu r^2}=E \]

It is important for us to talk about one more thing before we start setting up and doing our simulation: bounded orbits versus unbounded orbits. Intuitively, two masses will orbit each other if the total energy is not too high or too low. How do we determine whether the orbit will be bounded? Let's draw a plot of the total energy:

Here, as you can see, for \(E\lt;0\) we get an unbounded orbit, but for \(E \gt; 0\) we get a bounded orbit. Because total energy is conserved, it cannot suddenly increase, so the motion remains confined. In this case the trajectory is an ellipce for an inverse-square force. \(\textbf{The conclution is: if the total energy is less than zero, it's a bounded orbit and if not then it's unbounded}\)


Simulation & Analysis: Two-Body Problem

Here, we present two methods for simulating the two-body problem. The purpose of these simulations is to gain deeper insight into the system's dynamics. First, we will use the one-dimensional reduced two-body Lagrangian to run a simple simulation; then, we will switch to the full two-dimensional (x-y) coordinates to derive and simulate the full Lagrangian, allowing us to explore a wider range of two-body scenarios.

Setting Up the Simulation: Equivalent One-Dimensional Problem

Let the two masses be m1 and m2. In the one-dimensional reduced formulation, we assume that the center of mass is fixed at the origin. Therefore, the Lagrangian we have to work with is: \[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\mu \dot{\mathbf{r}}^2 - U_{\text{eff}}(r) \] where \(U_{\text{eff}}\) is: \[ U_{\text{eff}} = U(r) + \frac{l^2}{2\mu r^2} = \frac{-\gamma}{|\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2|} + \frac{l^2}{2\mu r^2} = U(r) + U_{\text{cf}}(r) \] So: \[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2}\mu \dot{\mathbf{r}}^2 + \frac{\gamma}{|\mathbf{r}_1 - \mathbf{r}_2|} - \frac{l^2}{2\mu r^2} \quad\text{where}\quad \boxed{\gamma = G m_1 m_2} \]

Before we start the simulation we need some initial conditions. We can choose a set of initial conditions such that the orbit is a perfect ellipse with period \[ T^2 = 4\pi^2 \frac{a^3 \mu}{\gamma}. \] Okay, let's do that.

The initial conditions we need are \(\mathbf{r}\) and \(\dot{\mathbf{r}}\). Our simulations are going to be on the Cartesian plane, so first let us represent the initial conditions in terms of (x–y) variables; then we can calculate \(\mathbf{r}\) and \(\dot{\mathbf{r}}\) from there.

Let the eccentricity of the orbit be \(e\) and the semi-major axis \(a\). Then the perihelion distance is: \[ r_p = a(1 - e) \] and the relative velocity required for two objects to orbit each other with orbital eccentricity \(e\) is: \[ v_p = \sqrt{G M \!\left(\frac{2}{r_p} - \frac{1}{a}\right)}. \] We have seen that \[ r_1 = \frac{m_2}{M} r \quad\text{and}\quad r_2 = -\frac{m_1}{M} r. \] So on the Cartesian plane, the initial positions are: \[ x_{10} = \frac{m_2}{M} r_p \quad;\quad x_{20} = -\frac{m_1}{M} r_p \] \[ y_{10} = 0 \quad;\quad y_{20} = 0 \] and the velocities are: \[ vx_{10} = 0 \quad;\quad vx_{20} = 0 \] \[ vy_{10} = \frac{m_2}{M} v_p \quad;\quad vy_{20} = -\frac{m_2}{M} v_p. \] We also previously calculated initial anguler momentum is: \[ l_0= \mu \bigl[(x_{10} - x_{20})(v_{y_{10}}-v_{y_{20}}) - (y_{10} - y_{20})(v_{x_{10}}-v_{x_{20}})\bigr] \] That's all the initial conditions we need. Now we can start writing the code.

Okay, before we start coding, we’ll nondimensionalize (scale) our variables so that all lengths, times, and masses become unitless. We will use Earth’s mass and Earth’s orbital distance as the standard mass and length. Our unitless variables for mass, distance, and time are: \[ m'_{i} = \frac{m_i}{M}, \quad\quad r' = \frac{r}{L}, \quad\text{and}\quad t' = \frac{t}{t_0} \] Here \(m'_{i}\) is the unitless mass, \(r'\) is the unitless distance, and \(t'\) is the unitless time. We still need to choose \(t_0\) so that the constant (the equivalent of \(G\)) in our dimensionless equations has a value of \(1\). Now that we have the initial conditions, we can numerically solve the two-body problem:

Python 
 #Time and time steps
 t_prime=2*np.pi*np.sqrt(a**3/(M))
 t0=np.sqrt(AU**3/(G*M_s)) 
 t = np.linspace(0, t_prime, num=100000)
 #Equation of motion
 def dSdt(S, t):
     x1, y1, x2, y2, vx1, vy1, vx2, vy2 = S

     # displacement vector from 1 → 2
     dx = x2 - x1
     dy = y2 - y1
     r3 = (dx**2 + dy**2)**1.5

     # accelerations
     ax1 =  m2 * dx / r3       # mass 1 pulled toward mass 2
     ay1 =  m2 * dy / r3
     ax2 = -m1 * dx / r3       # mass 2 pulled toward mass 1
     ay2 = -m1 * dy / r3 

     return [
         vx1, vy1,         # derivatives of x1, y1
         vx2, vy2,         # derivatives of x2, y2
         ax1, ay1,         # derivatives of vx1, vy1
         ax2, ay2          # derivatives of vx2, vy2
     ]

 #Solution
 y0 = [x1_0, y1_0, x2_0, y2_0, vx1_0, vy1_0, vx2_0, vy2_0]
 sol = odeint(dSdt, y0, t)

Let’s update the initial conditions as if we had a planet or star and a celestial object was caught by its gravitational field. In that case, how would it look? Most importantly, what would the energy versus r plot look like, and what kind of orbit would it form?

The escape velocity \(v_{\mathrm{esc}}\) from a spherically symmetric mass \(M\), measured at a distance \(r\) from its center (neglecting any atmosphere or other forces), is given by \begin{equation} v_{\mathrm{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}} \ \end{equation} where \(G\) is the gravitational constant, \(M\) is the mass of the body, \(r\) is the radial distance from its center.

So, to get a bounded orbit our initial condition for velocity must be less then the escape velocity. Here I use the following initial condition:

Python

Changes of Orbit

Alright, here we will discuss changing orbits. Imagine a scenario: if we have an object orbiting a planet and need to change its orbit for a certain reason. One example I can give is: let’s say we have a satellite orbiting close to Earth in a circular orbit, and we want the satellite to reach Venus. To do that, we have to alter the satellite’s orbit so that it intersects Venus’s path around the Sun. Here we will consider a satellite that changes from one orbit to another by firing its rocket tangentially—either forward or backward—when it is at the perigee of its initial orbit.

We have solved the differential equation, we obtain the orbital equation in the form, \[ r(\phi)=\frac{c}{1+\epsilon\cos(\phi)} \]

Here, \(c = \frac{l^2}{\mu\gamma}\).

Now consider two orbits with energies \(E_1\) and \(E_2\), as shown below:

The equations of the orbits depicted in the image are: \[ r_1 \left(\phi\right) = \frac{c_1}{1+\epsilon_1\cos(\phi)} \quad \text{and} \quad r_2 \left(\phi\right) = \frac{c_2}{1+\epsilon_2\cos(\phi+\delta)} \]

Example

Here, I want to discuss a simple example and show how we change orbit. Consider we have a satellite orbiting a planet in a circular orbit, and we would like to change its orbit to an elliptical one so that the planet is at the focus of the orbit.

Thrust factor, \(\lambda\)

We shall introduce the \(\textbf{thrust factor,}\lambda\): the ratio of the satellite’s speed immediately before rocket firing to its speed immediately afterward. If the thrust factor \(\lambda \gt 1\) then thrust was forward and the satellite speeds up, but if the thrust factor \(0 \lt \lambda \lt 1\) then then the thrust was backward and the satellite slows down. One thing I should mention is that if we want to go to an orbit with a specific semi-major axis and eccentricity, then we have to at least fire our rocket twice, but there are certain cases where firing the rocket once is enough. For example, if a satellite was orbiting Earth in a circular orbit and wanted to go to a new orbit with eccentricity \(e=0.5\) and semi-major axis \(a=2\) units from a circular orbit with radius \(r=a=1\).

So, going back to our example, we will be needing two boosts to get to our desired orbit and hence two boost factors. In order to calculate the boost factors we first have to figure out what the velocities should be. Here, I want to consider a simple scenario: we have a satellite orbiting a planet, and after it completes one revolution around the planet it will fire its rocket once to go to the \(\textbf{Transfer Orbit}\) (a transfer orbit is any orbital path specifically chosen to carry a spacecraft or object from one orbit to another). Then, after it completes half of that orbit, we fire the rocket again and proceed to our desired orbit. The diagram below should help you imagine the scenario.

From the diagram one can easily compute \(a\), let \(r_1\) be the radius of the initial (circular) orbit, and let \(r_2\) be the periapsis distance of both the transfer ellipse and the final target orbit. Then for the transfer ellipse we have by definition of an ellipse (with focus at the force center): \[ r_p = a\,(1 - e) = r_2, \qquad r_a = a\,(1 + e) = r_1. \]

Adding these two equations, \[ r_p + r_a = a\bigl[(1 - e) + (1 + e)\bigr] = 2a \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; a = \frac{r_p + r_a}{2} = \frac{r_2 + r_1}{2}. \] Hence the semi‐major axis of our transfer orbit is \[ \boxed{a = \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2}.} \]

If you also need the eccentricity, subtract the two original equations: \[ r_a - r_p = a\bigl[(1 + e) - (1 - e)\bigr] = 2a\,e \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; e = \frac{r_a - r_p}{2a} = \frac{r_1 - r_2}{r_1 + r_2}. \]

Alright, so as we said, our satellite would move from a circular orbit to an elliptical one as you can see in the figure. So for that, the initial velocities are going to be — for the circular orbit \[ v_1=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_1}} \] Since \(e_1=0\) and \(r_1=a_1(1-e_1)\), we just have \(r_1=a_1\) and therefore \[ v_1=\sqrt{GM\left(\frac{2}{r_1}-\frac{1}{a_1}\right)}=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_1}}. \] Similarly, we can calculate the velocity needed for the transfer orbit, \[ v_t=\sqrt{GM\left(\frac{2}{r_t}-\frac{1}{a_t}\right)} \] where \[ a_t=\frac{r_1+r_2}{2}, \quad e_t=\frac{r_1 - r_2}{r_1 + r_2}, \quad\text{and}\quad r_t=r_1. \] The transfer orbit takes us to the point where we fire our rocket for the second (and final) time so that we can acquire the necessary speed to reach our desired orbit. We fire the rocket immediately after we reach halfway between, once the transfer orbit completes its half-revolution. Now that we have all the initial conditions and we somewhat have a good idea how to model and solve the system, we can simulate this numerically to verify our understanding and refine it.
